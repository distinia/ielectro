Laocitia, officially the Republic of Laocitia, is a sovereign state located in western Edrobe, bordered by Kashiria to the southwest, Metosia to the northwest, Alveria to the north, and Destenia to the east. It also possesses maritime borders along the Gulf of Kosher to the south and the Northern Sea to the north. With an area of 97,324 km² and a population of 8.3 million, Laocitia is known for its geographic diversity, ranging from vast taiga forests and the Yalmin Plain to the Laocitian Mountains, home to Sadut Mountain, and Labaro Lake, the largest freshwater lake in Edrobe.

Laocitia is a unitary parliamentary republic governed under a constitution adopted following the National Revolution. The head of state is the President, currently Borys Hellands, while executive power rests with the Prime Minister, Oliwia Brownosky, and the National Assembly serves as the unicameral legislature. The country is divided into four provinces—Saduckie, Jeziorne, Północne, and Południowe—as well as the Capital District of Dior. Laocitia is considered the most democratic country in the world, operating a competitive multiparty system with frequent alternation of power, high civic engagement, and full protections for civil liberties and constitutional rights.

The population is 87% ethnic Laocitian, with 9% Destenians and 4% other minorities, largely integrated into a civic-national identity framework. The official and only language is Laocitian, used exclusively in public life. The state enforces a strict monolingual policy while promoting high literacy and education standards. The population is largely urban (78%) and aging, with a median age of 41 and a life expectancy of 81 years. Immigration is minimal due to restrictive naturalization laws, and the country maintains a stable demographic profile through robust healthcare, public services, and family policy.

Religiously, Laocitia is predominantly Catholic (86%), with 10% identifying as non-religious and 4% adhering to other beliefs. The Catholic Church plays a prominent cultural and charitable role but holds no political power. The state is strictly secular, guaranteeing religious freedom while maintaining full institutional neutrality.

Laocitia's economy is highly developed and environmentally conscious, with a GDP of 1.1 trillion DTR, ranking 6th globally. It emphasizes domestic food self-sufficiency, importing select goods from Destenia. Its industrial sector is powered by hydroelectric energy from Labaro Lake, making Laocitia a regional leader in clean energy and energy exports. The country is also a hub of digital infrastructure, innovation, and high-tech industry, with significant public investment in green technology and automation.

In foreign affairs, Laocitia is renowned for its strict neutrality, rejecting military alliances and foreign bases. It is a founding member of the World Union and actively participates in the Edrobean Union, the Global Reserve Fund, and other multilateral organizations. While formally non-aligned, its closest partner is Destenia, with whom it shares deep economic and political ties.

Defense is managed by the Laocitian Defense Forces, composed of the Army, Air Force, and Coast Guard, all organized for purely defensive purposes. Law enforcement is centralized, rights-based, and operates under the authority of the Ministry of Justice and Public Order, with a national police force supported by independent oversight and a strong judiciary.

Laocitia is widely viewed as a model of democratic governance, social stability, and environmental leadership, blending a rich cultural heritage with a forward-looking vision grounded in neutrality, republican values, and sustainable development.

Etymology

The name Laocitia finds its roots in the ancient Laocitic word “Lauketz,” meaning “people of the lake,” a direct reference to the region’s long-standing cultural and spiritual connection to Labaro Lake, the largest freshwater body in Edrobe and the geographical heart of the nation. Over centuries, Lauketz evolved linguistically into Laocitia, solidifying during the classical period as a term for both the land and its people. The lake, seen not only as a source of water but as a symbol of endurance and national unity, gave birth to the poetic expression “Our Lake shall defend us,” which would later become the country’s national anthem.

Etymologically, Laocitia reflects a fusion of geography and identity, tying the physical landscape to a collective sense of belonging. The modern use of the name is inseparable from the country’s revolutionary history, as the term Laocitian came to signify those who rose during the National Revolution to reclaim sovereignty and democratic rule. In both historical and contemporary usage, “Laocitia” embodies the ideals of Union, endurance, and territorial pride, grounded in the enduring relationship between the Laocitian people and the natural world that sustains them.

Geography

Laocitia is situated in western Edrobe, and its geography is defined by a harmonious blend of forests, plains, mountains, and inland waters, offering a landscape of striking diversity within a relatively compact territory of 97,324 square kilometers. The country is characterized by expansive taiga forests in the north and northeast, most notably the Venius Forest, which covers a significant portion of the upland terrain and serves as a vital ecological zone rich in coniferous biodiversity, wildlife, and seasonal snowfall.

To the south and central lowlands stretch the wide Yalmin Plain, a vast area of fertile soil and open grasslands, used extensively for agriculture and grazing. The plain contrasts sharply with the dramatic topography of the Laocitian Mountains, which rise across the central spine of the country. This mountain range not only divides Laocitia’s ecological zones but also holds the nation’s highest elevation, the Sadut Mountain, which ascends to 4,568 meters above sea level. Sadut and its surrounding highlands are known for their alpine terrain, sharp ridges, and glacial features, contributing to Laocitia’s water cycle and natural defense.

At the heart of the country lies Labaro Lake, the only lake in Laocitia and the largest in the Edrobean continent. This freshwater lake is both a geographical and symbolic centerpiece, supplying water for domestic and agricultural use, regulating local climate, and serving as a reference point for regional identity. Its broad surface, deep basin, and surrounding highlands create a unique microclimate and support a delicate aquatic ecosystem.

Flowing from the southern flanks of the Laocitian Mountains, the Fairt River is the sole and longest river in the country. It traces a winding path through valleys and plains, eventually draining into the Northern Sea. The river is essential to irrigation, ecology, and inland navigation, enabling agricultural production across the Yalmin Plain and connecting inland communities through its historical role as a natural transport corridor.

Laocitia is also shaped by its maritime edges. In the south, it is bounded by the Gulf of Kosher, a warm coastal sea that influences the region's weather patterns and provides ecological contrast to the colder northern latitudes. To the north, Laocitia faces the Northern Sea, a cooler, open sea that frames the country’s boreal edge and completes its northern boundary. These coastal features enhance Laocitia’s climatic diversity and link its inland landscapes to the broader maritime environment of Edrobe.

Politics

Government

Laocitia is governed as a unitary parliamentary republic, a system that centralizes authority while maintaining democratic representation through a robust institutional framework. The Constitution, adopted following the National Revolution, provides the legal and political foundation of the modern republic, outlining the separation of powers between the executive, legislative, and judicial branches, and reinforcing the state’s commitment to republicanism, rule of law, and civic participation.

At the apex of the state structure is the President, who serves as head of state. The presidency in Laocitia is largely ceremonial, with limited executive powers, but it carries symbolic and diplomatic significance. The President is elected by the National Assembly and represents national unity, signs legislation into law, appoints the Prime Minister (subject to parliamentary confirmation), and oversees official state ceremonies.

The executive authority is exercised by the Prime Minister, who functions as the head of government and holds substantial control over national administration and policy implementation. The Prime Minister is typically the leader of the majority party or coalition in the legislature and governs with the support of the Council of Ministers, which consists of department heads responsible for the daily operations of government across fields such as finance, defense, education, and infrastructure.

Legislative power resides in the National Assembly, Laocitia’s unicameral parliament, which is directly elected by the people through proportional representation. The Assembly drafts and passes laws, approves the national budget, supervises the government, and ratifies international treaties. It also has the authority to pass motions of confidence or no confidence, making the Prime Minister and their cabinet directly accountable to the legislature. The Speaker of the Assembly presides over parliamentary sessions, ensuring the orderly conduct of legislative work.

Laocitia’s judiciary is independent and constitutionally protected. The highest judicial authority is the Supreme Court, which has the power to interpret the constitution, rule on the legality of laws and executive actions, and resolve disputes involving state institutions. Lower courts handle civil, criminal, and administrative matters, and judicial appointments are made through a merit-based process that involves both legislative oversight and legal expertise.

Political parties

Laocitia is widely regarded as the most democratic country in the world, a status earned through its deep commitment to pluralism, transparent governance, civil liberties, and electoral integrity. The political landscape is vibrant and competitive, characterized by a multi-party system that encourages ideological diversity, regional representation, and grassroots participation. Elections are held regularly, are monitored by independent institutions, and feature high voter turnout, reflecting a population that is highly engaged in the civic life of the republic.

There is no dominant ruling party, and power frequently alternates between parties or coalitions through peaceful democratic transitions. The National Assembly, Laocitia’s unicameral legislature, is composed of representatives elected via a proportional representation system, which ensures that even smaller parties can gain seats and influence policy. Political parties in Laocitia range across the ideological spectrum—liberals, conservatives, environmentalists, socialists, centrists, federalists, and independents all find space in the democratic arena. The country’s largest and most influential parties typically include:

  • The Laocitian Social Party – a center-left force promoting social equity, labor rights, public healthcare, and inclusive governance. It often forms coalitions with green or progressive parties.
  • The National Civic Union – a center-right party favoring economic liberalism, fiscal conservatism, and constitutional nationalism, with strong support among rural voters and traditionalist segments.
  • The Ecological Democratic Front – an environmentally focused party advocating for climate policy, green energy transition, and sustainable agriculture, particularly strong in mountainous and forested regions.
  • The Republican Renewal Movement – a centrist party committed to technocratic governance, administrative reform, and digital innovation, often playing a kingmaker role in coalition governments.

In addition to these, minority and regional parties, such as the Labaro Civic Forum or the Sadut Peoples’ Alliance, occasionally gain local influence and contribute to policy debates, especially in the areas surrounding Labaro Lake and the Laocitian Mountains.

Political life is further enriched by a robust civil society, independent media, public broadcasting, and open debate culture. Party financing is regulated, campaign transparency is enforced, and all parties must adhere to constitutional democratic norms. There are no banned political ideologies so long as they operate within the legal framework and uphold the principles of democracy and non-violence. Ultimately, Laocitia’s political party system reflects its identity as a model republic, where freedom of association, peaceful competition, and institutional fairness create an environment in which democracy is not only practiced, but deeply embedded in the national consciousness.

Administrative subdivisions

Laocitia is administratively organized as a unitary state with a centralized governance structure, divided into four provinces and a separate capital district, each functioning as key territorial units for public administration, policy implementation, and local coordination. While all authority ultimately stems from the national government in Dior, these subdivisions serve to decentralize service delivery, manage infrastructure, and facilitate civic participation at the regional level.

The four provinces are each overseen by a provincial governor, appointed by the central government but working in close coordination with locally elected provincial councils. These councils advise on regional development plans, supervise public utilities, and implement national policies tailored to local needs. The provinces do not possess legislative autonomy, but they operate with administrative competence in areas such as healthcare, education, transport, and environmental management. The four provinces of Laocitia are:

  • Saduckie – Located in the central mountainous region, named after Sadut Mountain, and known for its alpine landscapes and strategic water reserves.
  • Jeziorne – Encompassing Labaro Lake and its surrounding plains, this province holds ecological, cultural, and agricultural importance.
  • Północne – The northern province, adjacent to the Northern Sea, characterized by taiga forests, fisheries, and seasonal tourism.
  • Południowe – The southern province, stretching to the Gulf of Kosher, with coastal plains, port infrastructure, and a mild maritime climate.

Separately, the Capital District of Dior is administered independently from the provinces. It houses all major national institutions, including the National Assembly, Presidential Office, Supreme Court, and Ministry headquarters. The district is governed by a mayor and a city council, but also hosts federal agencies that ensure coordination between national and regional levels of government.

Together, this administrative model ensures coherent national governance, efficient public service delivery, and a high degree of civic accessibility, while preserving Laocitia’s unitary character and reinforcing the responsiveness of the state to local and regional dynamics.

Foreign relations

Laocitia is widely recognized as the most neutralist country in the world, a position that defines its foreign policy ethos and underpins its international identity. Rooted in the legacy of its National Revolution and constitutional founding principles, Laocitia's foreign relations are guided by the values of non-alignment, sovereignty, peaceful coexistence, and multilateral cooperation. The country maintains no military alliances, hosts no foreign bases, and strictly avoids entanglement in geopolitical rivalries, regardless of ideological or economic pressure.

This firm stance of principled neutrality does not equate to isolation. On the contrary, Laocitia is an active diplomatic actor and a founding member of the United Nations, participating vigorously in global forums such as the Global Reserve Fund, International Tribunal for Justice, World Trade Network, Global Health Organization, and the International Energy Agency. It also plays a respected role within the Edrobean Union, where it advocates for diplomatic resolution of conflicts, rule-based international order, and equitable economic partnerships across Edrobe.

Despite its commitment to neutrality, Laocitia maintains a deep and strategic bilateral relationship with Destenia, which is widely seen as its closest ally. This relationship is based not on defense cooperation but on economic integration, scientific collaboration, and political dialogue. Laocitia benefits from Destenian investment in infrastructure, energy systems, and digital technology, while also engaging in cultural and academic exchanges that foster mutual respect and interdependence. The two nations frequently coordinate on regional policy within the Edrobean Union and share similar views on climate policy, economic regulation, and institutional reform.

Laocitia also maintains cordial and constructive relations with all neighboring countries, including Kashiria, Metosia, and Alveria, pursuing cross-border cooperation in fields such as water management, environmental protection, and transportation. It has no territorial disputes and is often sought out as a mediator in regional tensions, particularly within Edrobe and occasionally between Edrobean and Tayanusan actors. The country’s diplomatic corps is highly professional and frequently involved in negotiation, peace facilitation, and humanitarian coordination. Consistent with its neutrality, Laocitia maintains a balanced trade policy with partners across all global blocs, avoiding preferential agreements that could compromise its independence. Its embassies are present in all major global capitals, and the country is a strong supporter of international law, environmental diplomacy, and humanitarian aid.

Military

The Laocitian Defense Forces represent a compact, professional, and entirely defensive military structure, aligned with Laocitia’s constitutional doctrine of strict neutrality and non-aggression. The LDF exists solely to defend the territorial integrity, airspace, and maritime borders of the Republic of Laocitia, and is legally prohibited from engaging in foreign military operations, hosting foreign troops, or entering alliances that would compromise the country's sovereignty or neutrality. The LDF is composed of three coordinated branches:

  • Laocitian Army is the ground force component of the LDF, designed primarily for territorial defense and domestic emergency response. It is a small but highly trained force, equipped with light armored vehicles, precision artillery, and mobile anti-air systems suited to Laocitia’s mountainous and forested geography. The army operates under a doctrine of strategic deterrence and civil protection, focusing on rapid mobilization, disaster relief, border security, and support to civil authorities in cases of natural calamities or internal threats. Its personnel are mainly professional soldiers, supplemented by a voluntary civil reserve corps trained in defense and logistics.
  • Laocitian Air Force is tasked with the protection of national airspace, reconnaissance, and air support to ground and naval operations. Its fleet includes a modest number of multi-role interceptors, surveillance drones, and rotary-wing aircraft optimized for search and rescue and rapid deployment. Due to Laocitia’s strictly non-offensive military doctrine, the air force emphasizes defensive air patrols, air policing missions, and aerial surveillance, particularly in the vicinity of the Laocitian Mountains and national infrastructure zones. All aerial operations are coordinated in strict compliance with international airspace law.
  • Laocitian Navy is responsible for the defense and regulation of Laocitia’s maritime borders, particularly along the Gulf of Kosher and the Northern Sea, the Coast Guard is a civilian-military hybrid force under the joint command of the Ministry of Defense and the Ministry of the Interior. Its duties include search and rescue, anti-smuggling operations, maritime safety enforcement, environmental protection, and coastal monitoring. Equipped with fast patrol vessels, maritime drones, and radar stations, the Laocitian Coast Guard is one of the most technologically modern components of the LDF, ensuring compliance with Laocitia’s maritime neutrality and international obligations.

The entire Laocitian Defense Forces operate under the authority of the Ministry of Defense, and are commanded by the Chief of the General Staff, a non-partisan professional officer appointed by the government. The President, while serving as civilian Commander-in-Chief, exercises this role in symbolic and constitutional terms only.

The LDF does not maintain offensive weapon systems such as ballistic missiles or long-range bombers, and its budget is proportionally modest, reflecting the country’s security doctrine of restraint, deterrence, and non-provocation. Defense policy is focused on readiness, territorial resilience, and civil cooperation, with conscription abolished and replaced by a volunteer-based recruitment model emphasizing civic service, professionalism, and international humanitarian law training.

Law enforcement

Law enforcement in Laocitia is built on the principles of professionalism, transparency, and strict adherence to democratic and constitutional norms. The system is centralized under the Ministry of Justice, which oversees national coordination, legal compliance, and operational standards across the entire country. Policing in Laocitia is designed not only to ensure public safety and enforce the law, but also to protect civil liberties and democratic values, reflecting the country's deeply institutionalized rule of law.

The main agency responsible for internal security is the Laocitian National Police, a civilian force tasked with crime prevention, criminal investigations, public order, and emergency response. Officers are trained in de-escalation techniques, human rights law, and Union engagement, with a strong emphasis on non-violence, restraint, and proportionality. The National Police operates across all provinces and the capital district, with local precincts reporting both to regional commanders and the national headquarters in Dior. Within the National Police, specialized units handle distinct tags of law enforcement:

  • The Criminal Investigations Division is responsible for handling serious crimes, forensic analysis, and organized crime investigations.
  • The Cybercrime Division monitors online threats, fraud, and data protection violations.
  • The Public Order Division manages large gatherings, demonstrations, and national events, ensuring peaceful assembly while preventing disorder.
  • The Internal Affairs Division investigates complaints against police officers and enforces disciplinary standards.

In addition to the regular police, Laocitia operates a Judicial Police, responsible for the execution of court orders, transport of detainees, and protection of court premises. This corps works directly with the judiciary and maintains a distinct chain of command to ensure independence from political interference.

Border security and immigration enforcement fall under the jurisdiction of the Laocitian Border Police, a paramilitary unit that operates at land crossings, seaports, and airports. This force ensures compliance with immigration laws, prevents trafficking and smuggling, and safeguards the national frontier while coordinating closely with customs and civil authorities. Laocitia also has a National Anti-Corruption Agency, which monitors integrity within the public sector and law enforcement institutions. This agency operates independently of the executive branch and can launch investigations, conduct audits, and refer cases directly to prosecutors.

Economy

Laocitia possesses a balanced and strategically structured economy, shaped by its emphasis on self-reliance, sustainability, and technological advancement. While relatively modest in population and landmass, the country has emerged as a regional economic powerhouse, with a GDP of 1.1 trillion DTR, ranking 6th globally. Its economic model is characterized by state-guided development, innovation-driven investment, and a strong commitment to environmental stewardship and social equity.

The agricultural sector in Laocitia is primarily oriented toward national food self-sufficiency, rather than exports. Agricultural policy is focused on securing stable domestic production, reducing reliance on international markets, and ensuring equitable access to food across all regions of the country. Farms are modern, highly mechanized, and often organized as cooperatives. However, due to climatic and geographic limitations, particularly in mountainous and forested areas, Laocitia supplements its food supply with targeted imports from Destenia, its closest economic partner. This arrangement ensures a diverse, balanced, and resilient food system while allowing Laocitia to avoid overproduction and maintain ecological stability.

The industrial sector forms the backbone of Laocitia’s economy, with a strong foundation in engineering, machinery, green technology, and energy infrastructure. One of the defining features of Laocitia’s industrial success is its hydroelectric sector, which has been developed around Labaro Lake, the largest lake in Edrobe. Massive investment in hydropower infrastructure has allowed Laocitia to become self-sufficient in renewable energy, while also serving as a key energy exporter to neighboring Edrobean countries. The country’s energy grid is decentralized, efficient, and digitally monitored, serving as a model of clean energy transition and regional energy security.

Beyond energy, Laocitia’s industries include precision manufacturing, eco-engineering, medical technology, and sustainable construction materials. High environmental standards are enforced across all sectors, and the country has successfully branded itself as a hub of green industry and climate-resilient innovation. Public-private partnerships are common, with the state playing an active role in funding research, regulating competition, and guiding long-term economic planning.

Laocitia is also at the forefront of digital transformation, with widespread high-speed connectivity, nationwide e-governance, and strong investment in cybersecurity, artificial intelligence, and education technologies. Its communication infrastructure is among the most advanced in Edrobe, allowing for seamless interaction between public services, private enterprises, and the academic sector. The integration of technology into daily life, education, and administration has made Laocitia one of the most digitally literate and connected societies in the world.

Internationally, Laocitia maintains a stable and diversified trade profile, though it remains selective and value-driven in its external economic partnerships. It exports energy, industrial equipment, and technological services while importing high-end components, luxury goods, and certain agricultural products. Trade is conducted through transparent, regulated agreements, often with regional neighbors and under the oversight of multilateral organizations such as the World Trade Network and the Edrobean Union.

Demography

Laocitia has a population of approximately 8,321,735 inhabitants, making it one of the smaller countries in Edrobe by size, but one of the most socially balanced and demographically stable. The country is characterized by a high level of urbanization, strong public health indicators, low emigration, and a steadily aging but still productive population. Laocitian society is defined by social cohesion, widespread access to services, and a deeply embedded civic culture, all of which contribute to demographic resilience.

The median age in Laocitia is 41 years, reflecting a mature population with relatively low birth rates and a gradually increasing life expectancy. While the fertility rate stands at around 1.7 children per woman, below replacement level, the country has compensated with robust healthcare, strong family policies, and productivity-enhancing investments in education and technology. Life expectancy is among the highest in the region, averaging 81 years, with a well-documented gender gap favoring women by about five years. The aging of the population is carefully monitored by state institutions, and policies have been enacted to adapt pension systems, workplace flexibility, and elder care infrastructure to future demographic realities.

Urban areas account for more than 78% of the population, with major population centers clustered around Labaro Lake, the capital district of Dior, and the plains of the southern provinces. Urbanization has been managed through strategic planning, ensuring high standards of housing, clean transportation, and access to public services. Rural areas, while less densely populated, benefit from state subsidies aimed at preventing depopulation and maintaining traditional agricultural communities.

Laocitia has a low rate of immigration due to its restrictive naturalization laws and selective entry policies. Migration is tightly regulated, and the small foreign population consists mainly of professionals or regional migrants with long-term residence permits. Emigration is also rare; Laocitians tend to remain in the country due to its high quality of life, political stability, and strong welfare systems. As a result, Laocitia has maintained demographic continuity without relying on population inflows.

Education is universal, compulsory, and publicly funded, with a literacy rate of over 99%. The country also has one of the highest university attendance rates per capita in Edrobe, with particular emphasis on public service, environmental sciences, and engineering. Gender parity in education and employment is well established, and the state actively promotes youth participation in civic and professional life to counterbalance demographic aging.

Demographically, Laocitia exemplifies a modern, post-industrial society: aging but not declining, urban but socially rooted, and nationally unified but globally aware. Its population policies focus on sustainability, intergenerational equity, and long-term planning, ensuring that demographic trends support—not threaten—the republic’s social model, political stability, and economic vitality.

Language

The official and sole language of Laocitia is Laocitian, a refined and standardized language of the Edrobean linguistic family, deeply intertwined with the nation’s cultural identity, historical continuity, and civic life. It is used exclusively in government, education, media, judiciary, and public communication, forming a central pillar of the unitary state’s policy of linguistic cohesion and national unity. The state regards Laocitian not only as a medium of communication, but as a symbol of republican values, heritage, and the collective spirit of the Laocitian people.

From primary school through higher education, all instruction is conducted in Laocitian, with literacy rates approaching universal levels. The national curriculum includes classical literature, modern Laocitian linguistics, and rhetorical education, ensuring that every citizen is proficient in the written and spoken form. Mastery of the language is a prerequisite for holding public office, serving in the judiciary, or gaining employment in state institutions.

Laocitia maintains a strict monolingual policy, and no regional or minority languages are recognized within the constitutional framework. This reflects both the country’s high level of ethnic homogeneity—with 87% of the population identifying as ethnic Laocitians—and the political consensus around the role of Laocitian as a unifying civic language. While some bilingual individuals exist, particularly among the Destenian minority, only Laocitian is used in public life, and all other languages are restricted to private or informal settings.

The preservation and cultivation of Laocitian is managed by the National Language Institute, a prestigious body tasked with overseeing linguistic purity, vocabulary modernization, and language policy. The Institute regularly updates official dictionaries, grammar guidelines, and neologisms, especially in scientific and technological fields, ensuring that Laocitian remains a relevant and evolving language in the digital age.

Media, cinema, literature, and public broadcasting are entirely conducted in Laocitian, with foreign works either subtitled or dubbed under strict linguistic guidelines. The state provides subsidies for publishing in Laocitian and encourages cultural production that reinforces linguistic pride and national narrative.

Religion

Religion in Laocitia is characterized by a predominantly Christian population, with the vast majority—86% of citizens—adhering to the Catholic Church, which plays a significant cultural and historical role in Laocitian society. The country’s religious identity is deeply rooted in its national development, with Catholicism having accompanied the people of Laocitia through centuries of political change, including the period of the National Revolution, where it served as both a moral guide and a symbol of resistance against authoritarianism.

Despite its strong Catholic heritage, Laocitia is a secular state, with the Constitution firmly separating church and government. The Catholic Church holds no formal political authority, and all public institutions, including schools, courts, and the legislature, operate independently of religious influence. However, the Church remains a respected cultural institution, actively engaged in education, healthcare, social services, and Union outreach. Many of the country's most prestigious schools and charitable foundations are affiliated with Catholic orders, operating under state supervision but retaining their religious character.

Public holidays such as Christmas, Easter, and All Saints' Day are nationally observed, and Catholic traditions permeate many aspects of public life, from ceremonial events to Union festivals. The presence of Catholic symbolism in public architecture, memorials, and national ceremonies is widespread, reflecting the Church’s historic role in shaping Laocitian identity. Religious tolerance is also deeply embedded in Laocitian civic culture, and citizens are free to believe, not believe, or change their beliefs without discrimination or coercion.

A notable 10% of the population identifies as non-religious, a demographic that has grown slowly in recent decades, particularly in urban areas and among younger generations. Another 4% follow other faiths, including small Protestant, Orthodox, and secular-humanist communities. The state protects the religious freedom of all individuals and ensures that minority religious groups can worship, organize, and register without interference, provided they adhere to national laws and constitutional values.

Religious education in public schools is optional and non-denominational, focusing on comparative religion and ethical philosophy. Students may also opt out entirely. In private Catholic schools, religious instruction is more prominent, but still subject to oversight to ensure academic neutrality and respect for diversity.

The Catholic Church in Laocitia is led by the National Episcopal Council, which governs church affairs in coordination with the Vatican but retains a distinctive national character. Laocitian clergy are often seen not only as religious leaders but also as moral voices in public discourse, engaging in debates on social justice, ethical governance, and the protection of the poor.

Ethnicities

Laocitia is an ethnically cohesive country, with 87% of the population identifying as ethnic Laocitians, a group unified by shared ancestry, language, culture, and historical memory. Ethnic Laocitians form the foundation of the republic’s national identity, and their traditions, customs, and civic values are deeply embedded in the country’s institutions, social fabric, and public life. This majority population is spread uniformly across the territory, particularly concentrated in the provinces surrounding Labaro Lake and the central highlands.

The second-largest ethnic group in Laocitia are Destenians, who make up approximately 9% of the population. Their presence dates back to historical migration waves and border realignments, especially during periods of shifting territorial control. Most Destenians are fully integrated into Laocitian society, fluent in Laocitian, and active in both the private sector and public institutions. Although culturally distinct, they are generally well-assimilated and are represented in regional councils, civic organizations, and professional sectors. Destenians are often concentrated in urban areas and southern districts close to the Destenian border.

The remaining 4% consists of miscellaneous minorities, including small communities of Tayanusan and Litherian migrants, naturalized foreign professionals, and multi-ethnic families. These groups tend to be located in the capital district of Dior and certain port-access regions, where international exchange is more frequent. Their cultural presence is limited to private and Union settings, as Laocitia’s public institutions operate under a strict policy of linguistic and cultural uniformity.

Laocitia does not pursue multiculturalism as a state policy. Instead, it promotes a model of civic integration, where all citizens—regardless of ethnic background—are expected to adhere to Laocitian cultural norms, speak the national language, and participate in the shared civic culture. Ethnic identity is seen as a private matter, while national identity is framed as a unifying, inclusive concept based on citizenship and constitutional loyalty.

Discrimination based on ethnicity is prohibited by law, and ethnic minorities enjoy full civil rights and protections. However, immigration is tightly restricted, and naturalization processes are rigorous, reflecting the country’s emphasis on demographic cohesion and social stability. Public discourse rarely centers on ethnicity, as national unity is considered paramount in Laocitian political and cultural life.

In essence, Laocitia’s ethnic composition is stable, homogeneous, and tightly bound to its national identity, with small but integrated minority communities contributing to the republic’s civic pluralism within a framework of shared language, law, and loyalty to the Laocitian state.
