Sifalam, officially the Sultanate of Sifalam, is a unitary Islamic absolute monarchy located in southern Tayanusa, bordered by Cusea to the north, Azaria and the Blue Sea to the east, and surrounded by vast desert interior. With an area of 1,215,464 km² and a population of approximately 91.5 million, it is one of the region’s largest and most populous countries. The capital is Al-Bashir.

Sifalam is ruled by an all-powerful Sultan, who holds supreme authority over all aspects of governance, religion, and law. The political system permits no parties, elections, or dissent. A Consultative Assembly exists but serves only in an advisory capacity. The Sultanate enforces Sharia law exclusively, with all legislation and jurisprudence overseen by a royal-appointed religious council.

The state religion is Sunni Islam, practiced by 98% of the population. Religion is inseparable from governance, education, and public life. All laws, education, and daily routines are aligned with Islamic doctrine as interpreted by the palace and religious authorities. Minorities, including Christians and small tribal groups, comprise 2% and are permitted only limited private worship.

The official language is Tayanusan, used in all public, legal, and religious domains. Other languages are not permitted in formal contexts. Ethnically, the nation is 90% Sifalami, with minority populations required to assimilate fully into national and religious norms. Ethnic diversity is not recognized in law or public policy.

The economy, with a GDP of 770 billion DTR, is state-directed and based on oil, gas, phosphate, and a Sharia-compliant financial system. Trade and investment are limited to ideological allies. Most citizens work in the public sector, and private enterprise is tightly controlled. Agriculture is marginal, but state-funded food and religious welfare systems sustain the population.

Sifalam maintains a large, loyalty-based military—the Sifalami Armed Forces—focused on internal stability and defense of the throne. Military service is mandatory for men, and the armed forces operate closely with the monarchy and religious institutions.

Law enforcement is centralized under the National Police and internal intelligence, tasked with enforcing both civil order and Islamic morality. Surveillance, morality patrols, and strict penalties for dissent or deviation are part of daily governance.

In foreign affairs, Sifalam is closely aligned with Cusea and the wider Tayanusan Community, championing Pan-Tayanusanism, religious unity, and regional self-reliance. It avoids relations with secular or liberal states and upholds a foreign policy based on faith, sovereignty, and dynastic diplomacy.

Etymology

The name Sifalam is rooted in the ancient Tayanusan word *“Safalīm”*, which translates to “those who endure” or “the strong people”. Historically, the term was used to describe the early tribes of the central plateau who survived in harsh desert conditions through a combination of faith, discipline, and communal loyalty. Over centuries, the term evolved into Sifalam, coming to symbolize not just physical endurance but spiritual resilience and divine allegiance.

The name was formally adopted with the establishment of the modern sultanate in 2020, emphasizing the country’s unbroken link to Islamic history, its absolute monarchy, and the belief that sovereignty is derived not from human law, but from God’s command. The official use of “Sultanate of Sifalam” in legal and international documents reflects this worldview—projecting a nation whose strength lies in its submission to divine authority and its preservation of ancestral unity.

Geography

The Sultanate of Sifalam occupies a vast and geostrategically important area in southern Tayanusa, bordered by Cusea to the north, Azaria and the Blue Sea to the east, and surrounded by expansive desert terrain on all other fronts. With a total area of 1,215,464 km², it is one of the largest countries in the region, combining rugged isolation with strategic coastal access.

The eastern region of Sifalam is defined by the Qalhad High Plains, an expansive elevated plateau stretching from the Azarian border toward the central interior. The Qalhad region is cooler, wind-swept, and dotted with rocky outcrops, dry wadis, and tribal settlements. It has historically served as a base of nomadic civilization, religious scholarship, and military training camps due to its defensible elevation and access to seasonal rain-fed pastures.

The rest of the country is dominated by the Sifalami Desert, a vast expanse of sand seas, stone valleys, and salt basins, including the Ramlah al-Kubra, one of the largest uninterrupted dune fields on the continent. This harsh environment has shaped the Sifalami national ethos of endurance, discipline, and loyalty to faith. Settlements in the desert are clustered around oases, military posts, and logistics corridors that connect the interior with the coasts.

Sifalam is also home to several rivers, vital for both agriculture and internal connectivity. The most significant is the Arvista River, which forms a natural boundary with Cusea and supports farming towns along its southern banks. Other notable rivers include the Nahr al-Bayid, which crosses the Qalhad Plain in the east and is partially dammed for water storage, and the Wadi Al-Mahjin, a seasonal river flowing toward the coast during the rainy season.

Along the eastern seaboard, Sifalam has direct access to the Blue Sea, with a rugged coastline featuring natural harbors, cliffs, and saltwater marshes. Major ports such as Port Al-Nashir and Al-Khamrah handle most of the nation’s limited but strategic maritime trade, while coastal security installations monitor international waters and fishing zones.

Sifalam’s geography reflects its national identity: a land forged by desert struggle, ruled from the spiritual highlands, and bordered by both civilizational allies and natural frontiers. It is a realm of extremes—arid, vast, and sacred—where nature reinforces the state’s message of divine order and national obedience.

Politics

Government

Sifalam is governed as an absolute Islamic monarchy, with all authority vested in the person of the Sultan, who serves as head of state, commander-in-chief, religious guardian, and source of law. The country’s political structure is not based on democratic principles or a separation of powers, but on the belief that sovereignty belongs to God, and that the Sultan, exercises that authority on behalf of the people through divine legitimacy and ancestral right.

The Sultan holds unlimited executive, legislative, judicial, and religious powers. All decisions—whether related to national policy, appointments, foreign relations, or religious interpretation—are made by royal decree. The Sultan governs through a network of appointed officials, tribal chiefs, military commanders, and religious jurists who act in his name, under a strict hierarchy of obedience.

The Prime Minister is appointed directly by the Sultan and functions as a chief administrative officer, overseeing the daily management of ministries and the implementation of royal directives. Ministers are not elected but chosen for their loyalty, lineage, or service in the military and religious institutions. All ministers are accountable solely to the Sultan and may be removed at his discretion.

The Chief Justice oversees the judicial system, which is fully based on Sharia law, as interpreted and enforced by state-appointed religious courts. There is no civil law separate from religious doctrine. The Chief Justice heads a network of judges (*qadis*) who operate across the provinces, adjudicating matters of family law, criminal cases, inheritance, and public morality. Appeals are heard only with royal permission, and ultimate legal authority resides with the Sultan.

While Sifalam has a Consultative Assembly, it is a non-legislative body composed of tribal elders, clerics, and notable citizens appointed by the Sultan. Its role is advisory only; it may issue opinions or provide regional feedback but cannot draft, amend, or block laws. The Assembly meets at the Sultan’s request and has no standing authority outside of royal instruction.

Religious authority is integrated into every level of governance. The High Religious Council, appointed by the Sultan, regulates mosque activities, issues fatwas, supervises religious education, and reviews all new laws and policies for alignment with Islamic principles. All media, education, and civic institutions are expected to reflect the religious and moral guidance of the monarchy.

Political parties

Sifalam does not permit political parties in any form. As an absolute Islamic monarchy, the state views the concept of political pluralism as fundamentally incompatible with its religious foundations, monarchical structure, and doctrine of divine sovereignty. Authority in Sifalam flows exclusively from the Sultan, and governance is conducted through royal decree, tribal loyalty networks, and religious hierarchy—not electoral or ideological competition.

The Constitutional Charter of Governance, issued at the Sultanate’s founding in 2020, explicitly bans any attempt to form political parties, opposition movements, or secular organizations, declaring them as threats to national unity, Islamic order, and dynastic stability. Advocacy for representative democracy, liberal reform, or constitutional change is considered subversive and punishable under national security and blasphemy laws.

All public discourse is regulated by the Royal Council for National Morality, and expressions of dissent—whether through media, religion, or informal gatherings—are swiftly addressed by state security and religious enforcement agencies. Governance and representation in Sifalam occur through tribal consultations, religious councils, and loyalist advisory bodies, none of which are elected. Regional figures may advise the Sultan through the Consultative Assembly, but all members are handpicked by royal decree and serve without independent authority.

In this system, loyalty to the Sultan, religious obedience, and tribal allegiance replace political ideology. The regime promotes a unifying doctrine of “One Throne, One Faith, One People,” in which the existence of political factions is seen as both spiritually and socially divisive.

Administrative subdivisions

Sifalam is divided into twelve administrative provinces, known as *wilayat*, which serve as extensions of the Sultan’s authority rather than autonomous regions. These subdivisions are designed to ensure the efficient implementation of royal decrees, maintain religious and political discipline, and uphold tribal balance across the vast territory of the Sultanate.

Each *wilayah* is governed by a Wali (provincial governor), who is appointed directly by the Sultan. Walis are selected from among trusted tribal leaders, retired military officers, or loyal religious figures, and they report exclusively to the Royal Court in Al-Bashir. Their role is to enforce central directives, maintain security, supervise religious compliance, and manage infrastructure and administrative functions within their province.

Below the provincial level, the administrative system is further divided into districts (qadaa) and municipalities (baladiyat), each managed by local administrators or chiefs approved by the Wali and confirmed by the Ministry of the Interior. These officials coordinate public services such as schooling, basic health care, road maintenance, and market regulation, while also serving as local agents of surveillance and ideological conformity.

Religious oversight is fully integrated into the administrative hierarchy. Each *wilayah* is assigned a Regional Mufti, subordinate to the High Religious Council, who supervises mosque activity, Islamic education, and moral enforcement. In larger cities, Morality Units operate in tandem with municipal officials to monitor behavior and ensure alignment with Sharia.

There are no elected officials at any level of governance. All authority flows top-down from the Sultan, and provincial boundaries are periodically adjusted to reflect tribal dynamics, strategic needs, or population shifts as determined by royal decree.

The capital, Al-Bashir, is governed separately as a Royal District, administered by the Chamberlain of the Court, and houses all central institutions, including the royal palace, major ministries, the Grand Mosque, and the Supreme Court. It is considered the heart of the Sultanate and receives special funding and ceremonial priority.

Foreign relations

Sifalam pursues a foreign policy rooted in religious fraternity, dynastic diplomacy, and a strong commitment to Pan-Tayanusanism—the vision of deeper unity and cooperation among Tayanusan Islamic nations. The Sultanate maintains strict neutrality in global power struggles, but is highly active in fostering regional integration based on shared faith, language, and cultural heritage.

Its closest and most strategic ally is Cusea, with whom it shares not only a long land border but also deep tribal, religious, and ideological ties. The two countries engage in regular joint summits, military coordination, and infrastructure projects, particularly in the border provinces. Sifalam supports Cusea diplomatically in international forums and treats any threat to Cusean sovereignty as a matter of national concern.

Sifalam is also a vocal advocate of Pan-Tayanusanism, often spearheading initiatives within the Tayanusan Community that promote cultural alignment, religious unity, common economic frameworks, and coordinated defense doctrines among Islamic states in the region. It has proposed the creation of shared religious curricula, unified border standards, and interlinked rail and trade corridors across Tayanusa.

The Sultanate participates in numerous regional and global organizations, including the United Nations, Global Reserve Fund, International Tribunal for Justice, World Trade Network, Islamic Cooperation Organization, Global Health Organization, and the International Energy Agency. However, its engagement is carefully controlled, ensuring that foreign treaties, development partnerships, or aid programs do not conflict with Islamic principles or national sovereignty.

Relations with secular or liberal democracies are minimal and mostly transactional, focusing on limited imports of technology or medical supplies. Sifalam does not permit foreign cultural institutions, NGOs, or political consultants to operate on its soil, citing moral protection and ideological sovereignty. The Sultan personally directs foreign policy and frequently hosts religious leaders, Tayanusan monarchs, and strategic allies for ceremonial and political consultations. These high-profile events reinforce the country’s image as a moral and dynastic anchor in the Islamic world.

Military

Sifalam maintains one of the most ideologically loyal and monarchically integrated military forces in the Tayanusan world. Known as the Sifalami Armed Forces (SAF), the military is structured around the principle that defense of the nation is inseparable from defense of the Sultan and Islam. The RSAF is not just a security institution but a pillar of religious order, internal cohesion, and dynastic permanence.

The Sultan serves as Supreme Commander, with all ranks swearing personal loyalty to him. He personally appoints the Chief of General Staff, senior officers, and the heads of elite guard units. The military operates under the direct supervision of the Ministry of Defense, which ensures that every aspect of defense policy aligns with the Sultan’s authority and Islamic doctrine. The RSAF is composed of four core branches:

  • Sifalami Army: The largest component, tasked with border defense, internal deployment, and protection of religious and governmental institutions. Ground forces are stationed strategically across the desert and high plain regions, with fortified bases and along key trade corridors. Units are trained in desert warfare, counter-insurgency, and rapid response to civil unrest.
  • Sifalami Air Force serves as the aerial shield of the Sultanate, safeguarding national airspace, supporting ground operations, and projecting the will of the Sultan across the skies of Sifalam and beyond.Training emphasizes aerial discipline, night combat, and precision targeting, often conducted in coordination with allied air units from friendly states. Beyond combat, the RSAF-Air also undertakes humanitarian and religious missions, including pilgrim transport, medical evacuation, and disaster relief, reinforcing its dual identity as both a guardian of the realm and a servant of divine duty under the Sultan’s command.
  • Sifalami Navy: Though Sifalam has a modest coastline, this branch secures key ports along the Blue Sea and monitors maritime activity near the Azarian coast. Patrol boats and radar outposts provide surveillance, anti-smuggling operations, and support humanitarian missions during storms or evacuations.

Military service is mandatory for all male citizens between 18 and 25 years old, with most conscripts assigned to basic training, logistics, or internal security roles. Exceptional candidates may be selected for officer schools or assigned to the Guard Corps. Female military participation is not permitted under current religious law.

The SAF is equipped with a combination of domestically produced armaments, jointly developed systems with Cusea, and limited foreign imports from approved Islamic states. Military industries are state-owned and tied to broader religious and national development goals. The defense budget is substantial—approximately 6% of GDP—reflecting the importance of the military not only in defense but in infrastructure projects, emergency response, and national indoctrination. Military parades, religious oaths, and public exercises reinforce the army’s role as the armed soul of the Sultanate.

In foreign policy, the military does not engage in alliances, foreign bases, or peacekeeping. It remains entirely focused on domestic sovereignty, monarchical survival, and support for Pan-Tayanusan defense coordination when aligned with Sifalami interests. The SAF is viewed not just as a professional force, but as a sacred institution that embodies the will of the Sultan, the strength of the state, and the command of God. Its motto: *"To serve the Throne is to defend the Faith."*

Law enforcement

Law enforcement in the Sultanate of Sifalam is an extension of monarchical power and Islamic guardianship, operating under the authority of the Ministry of Interior. The policing system is designed not only to uphold civil law but to enforce moral discipline, religious compliance, and absolute loyalty to the Sultan.

The national police force, known as the Sifalami National Police, is organized into provincial and municipal directorates and is responsible for maintaining public order, enforcing royal decrees, and carrying out day-to-day policing duties such as crime investigation, traffic control, and the supervision of markets, mosques, and public spaces.

Alongside the police operates the Sifalami Intelligence Authority (RIA)—a powerful internal security agency directly overseen by the royal palace. The RIA monitors ideological dissent, anti-royal sentiment, foreign influence, and sectarian deviation. It runs an extensive domestic surveillance network, maintains blacklists of suspected dissidents, and coordinates with the judiciary to prosecute offenses against the throne or the faith.

Police training places heavy emphasis on religious law, national ideology, and obedience to the Sultan. Officers are trained in Sharia, ritual enforcement, and methods for crowd control, public speech management, and coordination with tribal and religious leaders. All senior officers must pass ideological vetting and are frequently rotated between provinces to prevent the emergence of local loyalties.

Justice is delivered through religious courts, with police responsible for executing verdicts, enforcing punishments, and ensuring public respect for court authority. Blasphemy, apostasy, and disobedience to royal orders are treated as grave crimes, often tried under special jurisdiction with closed hearings.

Public protests, unsanctioned gatherings, and political organization are strictly prohibited and quickly dispersed. The Sifalami police system does not tolerate activism, secular expression, or criticism of the royal family, and is known for its speed and severity in suppressing perceived threats. In Sifalam, law enforcement is not a neutral administrative tool—it is a sacral duty, operating with the mission to defend the faith, uphold the Sultan’s authority, and preserve the moral order of the Islamic kingdom. Its presence in daily life is both protective and disciplinary, ensuring that society remains obedient, orderly, and spiritually aligned with the vision of the monarchy.

Economy

The economy of Sifalam is a state-controlled, resource-rich system structured around Islamic principles and monarchic authority. With a GDP of 770 billion DTR, it ranks 10th globally, driven primarily by its vast energy reserves, religious infrastructure, and a tightly regulated internal market.

At the core of Sifalam’s wealth is its petroleum and natural gas sector, concentrated in the western and central desert regions. All extraction and export operations are managed by the Royal Energy Authority, and revenues are funneled into sovereign funds controlled by the palace. These resources finance national infrastructure, religious institutions, and the royal family's extensive network of patronage.

Sifalam also possesses large phosphate deposits, copper reserves, and a growing refining industry, which are gradually being developed through partnerships with allied Islamic nations, particularly Cusea. However, foreign ownership is forbidden—only joint ventures with majority royal or tribal oversight are permitted.

The country follows a strictly Sharia-compliant financial system. Interest-based banking is prohibited, and all economic transactions are structured through Islamic mechanisms like murabaha, mudarabah, and ijara. Zakat is mandatory and centrally collected by the Royal Treasury, redistributed to support social services, mosques, and education.

Agriculture is limited due to arid terrain, but state-backed irrigation projects in the eastern Qalhad High Plains have created zones of grain and date cultivation. Livestock farming, especially camel and sheep herding, remains a traditional sector heavily subsidized and symbolically important.

Employment is overwhelmingly centered on the public sector, including ministries, security forces, and religious institutions. Private enterprise exists but is highly regulated and often reserved for loyalist families or tribal elites. Most citizens rely on state stipends, religious endowments, or employment in monarchy-affiliated corporations.

Trade is primarily regional, focused on Tayanusan allies. The country exports energy, minerals, and textiles, while importing machinery, foodstuffs, and medical supplies. International trade is conducted through royal ports on the Blue Sea and overseen by customs agencies reporting directly to the palace.

The economy is not growth-oriented in the liberal sense; instead, it is designed to ensure social stability, religious harmony, and centralized wealth distribution. Development is seen as a tool for preserving the moral order, not disrupting it. All investment is filtered through ideological approval, and secular economic reforms are categorically rejected.

Demography

Sifalam is one of the most populous countries in Tayanusa, with a total population of approximately 32.5 million people. The demographic structure reflects a young, religious, and tightly regulated society, shaped by state policies emphasizing Islamic values, social discipline, and dynastic loyalty.

The median age is around 26 years, driven by a traditionally high fertility rate, early marriage customs, and strong religious-cultural encouragement of family formation. Large families are common, particularly in tribal and rural areas, and state incentives such as zakat-funded subsidies, housing aid, and free religious schooling promote population growth within the framework of faith and monarchy.

Gender roles are strictly defined. While women have high literacy rates, their participation in public life is minimal and highly regulated. Female employment is generally limited to gender-segregated education, healthcare, and religious administration. The public sphere—politics, military, judiciary—is dominated by men, in line with traditional Islamic jurisprudence.

Urbanization is rising, especially in cities like Al-Bashir, Port Al-Nashir, and Qalhad, but the majority of the population still lives in tribal communities, provincial towns, or state-planned settlements near resource zones. These communities are tightly integrated into the religious and administrative hierarchy, with tribal leaders acting as local enforcers of national loyalty and moral standards.

Education is universal and religiously structured. All students attend state-approved Islamic schools, where Qur’anic memorization, moral instruction, and national obedience are emphasized alongside basic literacy and arithmetic. Advanced education is available in theology, law, and military sciences, but access to secular or foreign curricula is prohibited.

Healthcare is state-funded and embedded with religious ethics. Hospitals are gender-segregated and supervised by the Ministry of Health in coordination with the Ministry of Religious Affairs. Public health campaigns focus on maternal care, child vaccination, and religiously appropriate medical practices.

The population is almost entirely composed of native-born citizens, as immigration is not permitted, and citizenship is granted only by royal decree, typically reserved for those of proven Islamic lineage and ideological loyalty. The state also tightly regulates movement within the country, particularly in regions deemed strategically sensitive or religiously significant.

Language

The official and only language of Sifalam is Tayanusan, which serves as the sole medium for government, education, religion, media, and law. It is considered not just a practical tool of communication, but a sacred symbol of national and religious identity, closely tied to the Qur'an and the historical legacy of Islamic rule. Fluency in Tayanusan is required for all forms of public participation. All state documents, legal proceedings, and educational materials are produced in standardized classical Tayanusan, with pronunciation, grammar, and orthography strictly regulated by the Royal Linguistic Authority, an institution under the Ministry of Religious and Cultural Affairs.

There is no recognition of minority or foreign languages in public life. Use of other languages—especially Western or Edrobean tongues—is discouraged and, in some contexts, restricted as culturally subversive. Even in scientific or technical education, loanwords are often replaced with Tayanusan equivalents. Language education begins at early childhood with Qur’anic memorization and continues with a national curriculum focused on religious literacy, rhetorical clarity, and ideological precision. Mastery of Tayanusan is not only a civic duty but a measure of moral and social fitness.

Religion

Religion in Sifalam is not merely a belief system—it is the foundation of statehood, law, and identity. The Sultanate is an Islamic absolute monarchy, where Islam (98% of the population) is not only the official religion but also the sole source of legislation, national ideology, and civic behavior. The state follows the Sunni Tayanusan tradition, which informs every aspect of life, from family law to foreign policy.

The Sultan is regarded as the Custodian of the Faith, both a temporal and religious leader whose authority is legitimized by divine will. All laws, court decisions, and policies must align with Sharia, as interpreted by the High Religious Council, a royal-appointed body of senior scholars and jurists.

Religious education is compulsory and deeply embedded in the school system. Children memorize the Qur’an from a young age, and all students are taught the principles of Islamic jurisprudence, public morality, and loyalty to the Sultan as protector of the ummah. Friday sermons are centrally written and distributed to every mosque in the country to ensure consistency in message and obedience.

Mosques are regulated by the Royal Ministry of Religious Affairs, which controls appointments of imams, religious publications, and the management of waqf (endowments). Independent religious activity is not permitted, and any unauthorized preaching, sectarian divergence, or doctrinal deviation is considered a threat to public order. The remaining 2% of the population, mostly small Christian or heterodox communities, may practice in strictly licensed private spaces but are excluded from public religious life, state institutions, and the education system. Proselytism, apostasy, or criticism of Islam is criminalized under national security law.

Public life is punctuated by Islamic rituals: the nation follows the Islamic lunar calendar, all government institutions close during Ramadan, and national celebrations are religious in nature. Zakat (almsgiving) is collected by the state and redistributed through official channels. In Sifalam, religion is not separate from politics or culture—it is the state itself. The role of Islam is to unite the people, legitimize the monarchy, and guide every action of governance, making it the indivisible essence of Sifalami national life.

Ethnicities

Sifalam is an ethnically homogenous nation, with Sifalamis comprising approximately 90% of the population. The Sifalami ethnic identity is deeply interwoven with the country’s Islamic character, linguistic uniformity, and loyalty to the royal family, forming the cornerstone of national unity and political stability.

The Sifalamis are traditionally desert-dwelling tribes whose historical legacy is closely tied to the founding of the Sultanate and the establishment of its absolute monarchy. Their identity is defined by tribal lineage, religious adherence, and linguistic purity in the Tayanusan language. Tribal affiliations still influence appointments in administration, the military, and religious offices, but all ethnic expression is subordinated to the ideals of national and Islamic cohesion.

The remaining 10% of the population includes minority groups of mixed ancestry, small nomadic clans, and coastal communities with partial foreign heritage, especially near the Blue Sea. These groups are fully subject to the state’s policy of assimilation, with no official recognition of distinct ethnic identities. They are expected to adopt the language, religious practice, and civic customs of the dominant majority.

Ethnic diversity is not acknowledged in official discourse. The government promotes the idea that all loyal citizens are one people under God and the Sultan, rejecting ethnic pluralism as a potential threat to national unity. Public references to ethnicity outside the national framework are discouraged, and ethnic-based organizations or cultural expression are prohibited by law.
