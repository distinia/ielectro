Azaria, officially the Kingdom of Azaria, is a unitary Islamic constitutional monarchy located in northeastern Tayanusa. It borders Lamberia to the north, Sifalam to the west, and the Sea of Ricene and Blue Sea to the east and north. Covering an area of 153,489 km², it has a population of approximately 49.3 million. The capital city is Azar, a spiritual, political, and economic hub.

The country is governed under a constitution adopted in 2020, blending Islamic law with monarchical authority and limited parliamentary representation. The head of state is King Ibrahim Al-Mahdi, who also serves as the guardian of Islam and commander-in-chief. The government is led by Prime Minister Khalid Al-Mahdi, with legislative power shared between a partially elected House of Representatives and a royally appointed House of Elders. The judiciary operates under Sharia, with a religiously guided legal system enforced by the General Attorney and national courts.

Azaria’s economy, valued at 520 billion DTR, ranks 12th globally. It is driven by natural gas, desert minerals, solar energy, and religious tourism, particularly around sacred cities and pilgrimage routes. The economy follows Islamic financial principles, with zakat, state subsidies, and bans on interest shaping its public and private sectors. The state plays a strong role in managing resources and guiding development under religious and national priorities.

The population is primarily Azari (79%), with significant Sifalami (17%) and Racabi (7%) minorities. The official language is Tayanusan, spoken by nearly the entire population. The society is religiously devout, with 73% identifying as Muslim, 19% Christian, and 8% belonging to other faiths. Islam defines the cultural and institutional identity of the state, influencing education, law, and social conduct.

Azaria has a professional military, the Royal Armed Forces of Azaria, designed for defense and internal order. It maintains a policy of non-alignment and religious sovereignty, while participating in multilateral organizations such as the United Nations, Islamic Cooperation Organization, Tayanusan Community, and International Energy Agency. Its foreign policy emphasizes Islamic unity, non-interference, and cautious economic cooperation.

The kingdom is divided into ten provinces, each governed by royally appointed governors. Law enforcement combines national police, religious officers, and intelligence agencies to uphold public order and religious compliance. Azaria promotes national unity through linguistic and religious assimilation, with all civic life oriented around Islamic and monarchical authority.

Azaria is widely recognized as a religiously guided, strategically positioned state that balances tradition with administrative control, shaping its society through a fusion of faith, discipline, and centralized governance.

Etymology

The name Azaria is believed to derive from the ancient Tayanusan root “Azar,” meaning “fire” or “radiance,” which historically symbolized divine light, wisdom, and sovereignty. The term was originally used to refer to the region surrounding the modern capital Azar, where early religious scholars, tribal leaders, and monarchs established the foundations of what would later become the Azarian kingdom.

In classical religious texts and early chronicles, “Land of Azar” was described as a spiritually blessed territory, often associated with prophecy, knowledge, and sacred law. Over time, the name evolved into “Azaria,” signifying not just a place, but an ideal: a nation guided by faith, ruled by divine law, and united under a righteous monarchy.

The modern state’s founders officially adopted the name Azaria in the 2020 Constitution to reflect this historical and spiritual continuity, as well as the country’s mission to uphold Islamic values, cultural heritage, and moral leadership in the Tayanusan region. Today, the name represents both national pride and a deep commitment to the country's religious and political traditions.

Geography

Azaria is located in northeastern Tayanusa, bordered to the north by Lamberia and the Blue Sea, to the east by the Sea of Ricene, and to the west by Sifalam. Its geographical position gives it access to both continental trade corridors and maritime routes, making it a critical link between inland Tayanusan economies and the broader Ricene region.

The country features a diverse but predominantly arid landscape, structured around three major ecological zones:

The northern region consists mainly of savannah biome, known locally as the Zarif Savannah, characterized by open grasslands, seasonal rivers, and scattered acacia trees. It is home to the fertile Raqim Plateau, a highland area that supports agriculture, pastoralism, and several urban settlements including the capital, Azar.

The central and southern territories are dominated by two vast desert regions:

  • The Dashem Desert, spanning much of the central interior, is marked by wide sand plains, rock formations, and intermittent oasis towns. It is sparsely populated but contains valuable mineral deposits and solar energy fields.
  • The Nadir Desert, located in the southeastern part of the country near the Sea of Ricene, features salt flats, ancient caravan routes, and seasonal wadis (dry riverbeds). It is known for its historic trade towns and spiritual retreats.

Despite its arid terrain, Azaria is traversed by several important rivers, the most notable being the Wadi Al-Malik, which originates in the north and flows intermittently toward the Blue Sea. Another seasonal watercourse, the Rafaq River, cuts through the eastern interior and supports limited agriculture and herding along its banks during the rainy months.

In the northwest lies Lake Sirwan, the largest inland water body in Azaria. Though shallow and saline, it serves as a critical ecological and economic resource, supporting fishing, water storage, and migratory bird habitats. Several towns and religious centers are located near its shores.

Politics

Government

Azaria is a unitary Islamic constitutional monarchy, blending religious authority, royal leadership, and parliamentary governance under a system that prioritizes both divine law (Sharia) and representative consultation (Shura). Established by the Constitution of 2020, Azaria’s political framework is built on the principles of faith, order, and justice, with clear roles for the monarchy, elected institutions, and religious jurisprudence.

The King of Azaria, currently Ibrahim Al-Mahdi, is the head of state, commander-in-chief, and the symbolic guardian of Islam and national unity. He holds substantial authority in foreign affairs, defense, religious oversight, and the appointment of key officials. The King also ratifies legislation and royal decrees, convenes the Parliament, and has the constitutional authority to dissolve it in times of crisis. His legitimacy is both hereditary and religiously endorsed, and his position is widely revered.

The Prime Minister, currently Khalid Al-Mahdi, serves as head of government, overseeing the Council of Ministers and the day-to-day administration of the state. The Prime Minister is appointed by the King, typically from the majority party or ruling coalition in the House of Representatives. He is responsible for implementing policy, supervising ministries, and managing internal affairs in accordance with both civil law and Islamic principles. The legislature is composed of two houses:

  • The House of Representatives (lower house) is partially elected by the public and partially appointed by the King. It debates and approves legislation, budgets, and government programs, and holds ministers accountable.
  • The House of Elders (upper house) is fully appointed by the King and consists of religious scholars, tribal leaders, former judges, and senior statesmen. It reviews legislation for religious conformity, constitutional compliance, and national interest. It also advises the monarchy on moral, cultural, and legal matters.

The General Attorney, currently Omar Farid, heads the Sharia-based judicial system, ensuring that all laws conform to Islamic jurisprudence. Courts operate at multiple levels, with the Supreme Sharia Court acting as the final legal authority. Judges are appointed by royal decree and are trained in both civil and Islamic law.

Although the monarchy remains central to Azaria’s governance, the Constitution guarantees limited political pluralism, popular representation, and a regulated legal framework. Political parties are permitted, provided they respect Islamic values and do not threaten national unity or religious order.

Azaria’s government is thus a hybrid system, rooted in faith and monarchy, yet open to democratic expression within a religiously guided constitutional framework—striking a balance between tradition, divine law, and modern administrative efficiency.

Political parties

Azaria permits political parties under strict constitutional and religious guidelines, allowing for regulated pluralism within an Islamic framework that prohibits sectarianism, atheistic ideology, and anti-monarchical agitation. All political organizations must affirm loyalty to the King, respect the Constitution, and uphold Islamic principles as the foundation of public life.

  • The country’s political landscape is dominated by the Faith and Nation Party, a conservative, pro-monarchy Islamist party that advocates for Sharia-compliant governance, social harmony, and economic nationalism. Closely aligned with the royal house and supported by many religious institutions, the FNP has consistently held a majority in the House of Representatives and provides most ministers in the government. It emphasizes moral legislation, subsidized welfare, and traditional education.
  • The main opposition comes from the Azarian Reform Alliance, a moderate Islamist and constitutionalist coalition that supports greater parliamentary powers, administrative decentralization, and economic liberalization—while firmly maintaining religious and monarchical loyalty. It draws support from younger urban voters, professionals, and reform-minded religious scholars.
  • The Social Justice Movement – A centrist religious party focused on anti-corruption, rural development, and tribal inclusion.
  • The Traditional Unity Front – A tribal-conservative bloc with strong support in the Sifalami and Racabi regions, advocating for cultural preservation and localized governance.

Parties that promote secularism, atheism, or ethnic separatism are banned under Article 8 of the Constitution. The Ministry of Religious and Political Affairs monitors party platforms and campaign rhetoric to ensure compliance with national values.

While political competition exists, it is conducted within clearly defined ideological and religious limits. Campaigns focus more on policy and representation than ideological confrontation. This model, though restrictive by liberal standards, is seen in Azaria as a faithful expression of Islamic democracy, aiming to preserve unity, order, and spiritual integrity while allowing popular participation in governance.

Administrative subdivisions

Azaria is administratively divided into ten provinces, known as wilayahs, each serving as a governorate under the central authority of the Royal Government. These wilayahs are designed to balance regional representation with strong national unity, and their boundaries reflect a combination of tribal territories, historical regions, and geographic zones.

Each wilayah is headed by a Governor (Wali), appointed directly by the King upon the recommendation of the Ministry of Interior and Religious Affairs. Governors are responsible for implementing national policy, overseeing local infrastructure, security coordination, and ensuring that religious standards and civil regulations are properly enforced at the provincial level. While not elected, governors often belong to influential local families or respected scholarly lineages, reinforcing trust and loyalty at the community level.

Below the provinces are districts (nahiyat) and municipalities (baladiyat), which handle local administration, including public works, education, water distribution, and municipal policing. These municipalities are managed by municipal councils, a portion of which are locally elected, with the rest appointed by the governor. Mayors (Ra’is al-Balad) are typically appointed for major cities, including Azar, Raqim, and Sirwan, and operate under close oversight from both the governor and the central government.

The capital, Azar, holds a special administrative status as a royal city, directly governed by the Crown Authority of Azar, a unique body appointed by the King and composed of senior royal advisors, religious leaders, and civilian administrators. This body manages the city's security, ceremonial functions, and coordination with national ministries.

While the state remains highly centralized, recent reforms have increased budgetary discretion and service autonomy for provincial and municipal governments, especially in infrastructure, education, and social programs. However, religious education, national security, and legal policy remain strictly centralized.

Foreign relations

Azaria’s foreign policy is rooted in the principles of Islamic solidarity, non-interference, and strategic neutrality, while maintaining a firm commitment to regional stability, cultural diplomacy, and religious identity. As a member of the Tayanusan Community, as well as global institutions such as the United Nations, Global Reserve Fund, International Tribunal for Justice, World Trade Network, and International Energy Agency, Azaria actively participates in multilateral diplomacy while preserving its religious and sovereign values.

The country’s leadership emphasizes the role of Azaria as a protector of Islamic morality and heritage, as member of the Islamic Cooperation Organization, seeking to promote peaceful coexistence, mutual respect, and economic cooperation across ideological and religious lines—provided they do not conflict with Azarian core principles. Azaria maintains strong bilateral relations with fellow Tayanusan nations, particularly Sifalam, Cusea, and Lamberia, with whom it shares deep religious, cultural, and linguistic ties. It also plays an influential role in the Islamic Association Organization, a faith-based diplomatic network that promotes dialogue among Islamic states and offers mediation services in inter-Muslim disputes.

While Azaria is cautious about aligning with secular or totalitarian regimes, it has maintained cordial diplomatic channels with Edrobean powers such as Destenia, Boravia, and Metosia, primarily through economic, energy, and infrastructure agreements. Its ports along the Sea of Ricene and Blue Sea make it a valued partner in regional shipping and trade logistics. Azaria’s foreign policy avoids military entanglements and foreign bases, upholding a strict doctrine of defensive sovereignty. It instead focuses on humanitarian aid, religious outreach, and educational exchange, often funding Islamic schools and scholarship programs in developing countries aligned with its worldview.

Military

Azaria maintains a compact but capable military force known as the Royal Armed Forces of Azaria, structured to serve defensive purposes, uphold internal stability, and enforce Islamic sovereignty within the framework of the Kingdom’s constitutional and religious principles. The military is under the supreme command of King Ibrahim Al-Mahdi, who serves as Commander-in-Chief, while day-to-day operations are managed by the Ministry of Defense and Religious Security Affairs. The RAFA consists of three main branches:

  • Royal Army – The largest component, responsible for territorial defense, border security, and domestic stability. It includes mechanized infantry, armored brigades, and elite desert units trained for operations in the Dashem and Nadir deserts. These forces are also tasked with protecting holy sites, government institutions, and infrastructure.
  • Royal Air Force – Focused on air surveillance, rapid response, and defensive airspace protection. It operates a fleet of light combat aircraft, drones, and transport helicopters, optimized for both urban and desert conditions. It also plays a role in emergency response and disaster relief.
  • Royal Navy – This branch secures the country’s coastline along the Blue Sea and Sea of Ricene, operating patrol vessels, radar stations, and maritime border checkpoints to combat smuggling and illegal entry.

Azaria follows a doctrine of strategic restraint, with military action reserved strictly for the defense of faith, homeland, and royal authority. It does not maintain foreign military alliances or allow foreign bases on its soil. However, the country participates in joint military drills with other Tayanusan nations, particularly Sifalam and Lamberia, focused on counterterrorism, border control, and religious site protection.

The Royal Military Academy in Azar provides officer training based on a curriculum that blends tactical instruction with religious ethics, and national loyalty. Service in the RAFA is voluntary, but military careers are respected and often pursued by those seeking state employment or higher education support. Military expenditure remains modest—about 2.1% of GDP—with investments focused on modernization, command infrastructure, and desert readiness, rather than force projection. Intelligence operations and domestic surveillance fall under the Internal Security Directorate, a parallel structure dedicated to counter-espionage and internal religious protection.

Law enforcement

Law enforcement in Azaria is organized under a centralized, religiously guided structure, combining national police services with a robust intelligence and internal security apparatus. These institutions function not only to enforce law and order but to safeguard Islamic principles, protect the monarchy, and ensure moral discipline in public life.

The national police are present in every province and operate under the supervision of the central government. Officers are trained in both criminal procedure and religious enforcement, reflecting Azaria’s dual legal system rooted in civil codes and Sharia. Patrols, investigations, traffic control, and public safety are handled through regional departments, with regular coordination between police and religious authorities. In cities like Azar and Sirwan, specialized units monitor compliance with moral conduct laws, including modesty codes, prayer enforcement, and bans on prohibited substances.

The country’s intelligence and internal security services are handled by the Directorate of Internal Protection, a powerful agency responsible for counterterrorism, anti-subversion, ideological monitoring, and protection of the monarchy. This agency conducts surveillance on individuals or groups suspected of activities that may undermine national unity, religious integrity, or the King’s authority. Intelligence officers also operate abroad, monitoring exiled opposition, foreign media, and ideological threats to Azaria’s cultural fabric.

Detention facilities and courts operate with strong oversight from both the Ministry of Justice, ensuring that punishments are legally and theologically justified. Public trials for major crimes are common, with proceedings framed as moral lessons for the broader community. While criticized by some international observers for restrictions on dissent, Azaria’s law enforcement institutions are regarded domestically as essential to social stability, religious purity, and obedience to divine order. They are deeply woven into the kingdom’s identity as a state of faith, law, and unshakable authority.

Economy

Azaria has a resource-driven and state-guided economy, shaped by its Islamic values, strategic geography, and emphasis on national self-reliance. With a GDP of 520 billion DTR, it ranks 12th globally, and plays a pivotal role in the Tayanusan economic bloc, particularly in energy production, religious tourism, and desert infrastructure development.

The backbone of the economy is the energy sector, especially the extraction and export of natural gas and minerals, concentrated in the Dashem and Nadir Desert zones. The state maintains full ownership over all energy resources, which are administered through the Royal Energy Authority. Profits from exports to Tayanusa and Edrobe are reinvested into infrastructure, housing, and public welfare—reflecting the Islamic principle of resource stewardship for the ummah.

A growing area of development is solar power, with vast photovoltaic fields constructed in the southern deserts as part of the national goal to achieve energy diversification and green sovereignty within the next two decades.

The agricultural sector is limited by arid conditions but supported by oasis farming, date cultivation, and state-subsidized irrigation projects near the Raqim Plateau. The government has invested in food security programs, including grain storage, halal livestock initiatives, and price controls during religious months.

Azaria also relies heavily on religious tourism, with millions of pilgrims visiting sacred sites in Azar, Raqim, and around Lake Sirwan each year. The state oversees this sector through the Ministry of Hajj and Religious Affairs, ensuring that accommodations, transportation, and religious services meet both spiritual and economic goals.

Small and medium-sized enterprises operate in sectors like construction, trade, textiles, and local transport, often supported through Sharia-compliant financing and Islamic banking institutions. Interest-based lending is forbidden, and the financial system operates under zakat obligations, profit-sharing models, and state-guaranteed risk protections.

Unemployment remains moderate, especially among youth, prompting initiatives for vocational training, artisan cooperatives, and state employment quotas for recent graduates. The public sector is the largest employer, especially in education, security, religious services, and infrastructure projects.

Azaria’s foreign trade policy is cautious, preferring bilateral agreements with trusted partners, particularly within the Tayanusan Community, and avoiding overexposure to secular or globalist markets. Imports are restricted in sensitive sectors, with priority given to domestic production, Islamic ethics, and cultural protection.

Demography

Azaria has a population of approximately 49,347,893, making it one of the most populous nations in the Tayanusan region. The population is predominantly young and rapidly urbanizing, with over 60% under the age of 35. This demographic profile is seen both as a strategic asset and a policy challenge, driving national investments in education, employment, and infrastructure.

The median age in Azaria is 29, and the fertility rate remains above replacement level, supported by a culture that encourages family formation, early marriage, and large households. Government programs provide incentives for marriage, housing, and childbirth, in line with both social policy and Islamic values.

Gender roles are defined by religious and legal tradition, with men primarily engaged in public affairs and women focused on family and community life, though female participation in education, healthcare, and religious service sectors is rising steadily. Women’s access to education has expanded significantly, especially in urban areas, but public roles remain limited by constitutional and religious restrictions.

Healthcare is universal and state-funded, particularly in maternal care, vaccination, and religious health ethics. Life expectancy is rising, and efforts to combat desert-related illnesses, malnutrition in remote areas, and chronic diseases are ongoing through the National Health Council.

Language

The official and sole language of Azaria is Tayanusan, with deep roots in the classical religious texts and tribal oral traditions of the region. It is spoken natively by nearly 100% of the population, and serves as the language of government, education, law, media, and religious instruction.

Tayanusan is regarded not only as a tool of communication, but as a symbol of national unity, religious identity, and moral clarity. The state considers linguistic uniformity essential for preserving Azarian Islamic values, and fluency in Tayanusan is mandatory for citizenship, public employment, and participation in any political or legal process.

Minority languages such as Sifalami and Racabi dialects are spoken privately or within tight-knit communities but are not recognized in public administration or education. Cultural use is tolerated so long as it does not challenge the primacy of Tayanusan or promote regional separatism. Government-run cultural centers sometimes allow the study of these languages in non-political contexts, often tied to poetry or oral history preservation.

The Ministry of Language and Religious Education oversees language policy, script standardization, and the publication of religious, legal, and civic literature. All schools follow a Tayanusan-only curriculum, with classical grammar and Quranic recitation forming the foundation of early education. Foreign language education is limited and tightly controlled. Some technical institutions offer basic instruction in Edrobean languages for trade and engineering, but emphasis remains firmly on Tayanusan as the language of virtue, law, and divine wisdom.

Religion

Religion is the foundation of public life and national identity in Azaria, where Islam is not only the majority faith but the guiding force of the Constitution, legislation, and governance. Approximately 73% of the population identifies as Muslim, predominantly Sunni, following the doctrines of the Azarian School of Jurisprudence, a branch of Tayanusan Islam characterized by its emphasis on social order, modesty, and divine sovereignty.

The King, as both political ruler and religious guardian, is entrusted with the protection of Islam and the enforcement of Sharia as the supreme legal authority. Religious law governs not only family and moral matters but also influences criminal justice, economics (through bans on usury), and public conduct. National holidays, education, and media all reflect Islamic teachings, and public rituals—such as prayers, fasting, and zakat—are enforced with state support.

The state ensures that Islamic practice remains visible and central. Mosques are state-funded and supervised by the High Council of Ulama, which certifies imams, regulates sermons, and issues religious rulings. Quranic education is mandatory in all schools, and religious broadcasts form a core part of national media programming.

19% of the population identifies as Christian, mainly from historical Tayanusan Orthodox and Eastern Catholic communities. These groups are allowed to worship freely in designated churches and maintain internal religious leadership, though they are excluded from senior public office and may not proselytize. Their schools and literature are subject to government review to ensure compliance with national values.

The remaining 8% includes smaller religious minorities such as tribal animists, Sufis, and unregistered sects. While personal belief is not criminalized, public deviation from Sunni orthodoxy, blasphemy, and acts of apostasy are penalized under the Religious Protection Code. Interfaith relations are regulated but stable, and the state often emphasizes tolerance within the bounds of Islamic supremacy. Religious diplomacy is a core pillar of foreign policy, with Azaria funding Quranic schools abroad, participating in inter-Islamic mediation, and promoting its vision of Islamic order and virtue.

Ethnicities

Azaria is an ethnically diverse Islamic monarchy, though united under a strong religious-national identity centered on Islam and Tayanusan culture. The population is predominantly Azari (79%), who form the linguistic, political, and religious core of the nation. Azaris speak Tayanusan natively and are deeply embedded in the monarchy’s historical and cultural foundations.

The Sifalamis (17%) are the largest minority group, residing mostly in the western provinces near the border with Sifalam. They share linguistic roots with Azaris but maintain distinct tribal customs, dress styles, and oral traditions. While fully Muslim, Sifalamis often follow localized interpretations of religious practice and have historically held positions in tribal leadership and provincial administration.

The Racabi (7%), mostly located along the Sea of Ricene, are historically coastal merchants and fishermen. They are known for their unique dialect, maritime culture, and involvement in trade and transport sectors. Though integrated religiously, Racabis retain distinct family and communal structures and often identify with specific coastal municipalities.

The remaining 5% includes small communities of Lamberians, Tayanusan Christians, and descendants of nomadic tribes who have gradually settled in urban areas. These groups live under the same legal and religious expectations as the rest of the population and are subject to the same linguistic and civic integration policies.

Azaria does not officially recognize ethnic classifications in its legal framework, as the Constitution emphasizes religious unity and national loyalty over ethnic origin. All citizens are expected to adopt Tayanusan as their primary language, adhere to Islamic law, and align with national customs. Ethnic minorities may maintain cultural traditions privately, but public expressions of ethnic separatism or linguistic deviation are discouraged.

The result is a society that is ethnically varied but culturally unified, where Islam and loyalty to the monarchy take precedence over ancestral identity. Ethnic cohesion is maintained not through pluralism, but through a model of religious assimilation and disciplined national unity.
