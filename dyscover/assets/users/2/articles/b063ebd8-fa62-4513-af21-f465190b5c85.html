Stasia, officially the State of Stasia, is a unitary Islamic presidential republic located in northern Tayanusa, bordered by Cusea to the west, the Blue Sea to the south and east, and the Grandic Ocean to the north. Covering an area of 120,478 km², it has a population of approximately 4.7 million. The capital and largest city is Nova Stasia, situated in the fertile western plains.

Established under a modern Islamic constitution, Stasia is governed through a centralized executive system led by a President, with a unicameral National Assembly, and an independent judiciary rooted in Sharia law. The government operates within a framework of strict religious discipline, state oversight, and national unity, with limited space for political opposition or regional autonomy.

The economy of Stasia, with a GDP of 370 billion DTR, is based on agriculture, desert minerals, and state-regulated trade and services. The fertile Al-Safin Plain and the irrigated lands around Nova Stasia form the core of its food production. The country enforces a Sharia-compliant financial system, and economic activity is largely concentrated in the public sector. Infrastructure, education, and health care are fully state-managed and ideologically aligned with religious and national objectives.

The population is 90% Stasian Muslim, with a homogeneous cultural identity built around the Tayanusan language, which is the only official and legally recognized language. Minority groups, including small Christian and tribal communities, make up the remaining 10% but have no formal role in public life. Islam is the state religion and shapes all legislation, civil conduct, and education. The judiciary is a dual system of civil and Sharia courts, with final authority resting in the Supreme Court.

Stasia maintains a professional, defensive military, focused on territorial integrity and internal order. It avoids military alliances and foreign bases, maintaining a policy of non-interventionism. Law enforcement is carried out by a centralized police system and a domestic intelligence agency, both tasked with maintaining religious discipline and state security.

In international affairs, Stasia is a member of the United Nations, Tayanusan Community, and several global organizations. Its foreign policy emphasizes religious alignment, neutrality, and limited trade partnerships, primarily with neighboring Islamic countries.

Stasia is characterized by its ideological cohesion, centralized governance, and faith-driven national identity, combining elements of modern statehood with a deep commitment to Islamic tradition and conservative societal order.

Etymology

The name Stasia originates from the ancient Tayanusan term *“Staysa”*, meaning “pillar” or “foundation”, historically used to describe the central highland region where the modern state was established. In classical literature and oral tradition, the word came to symbolize strength, spiritual endurance, and cultural prominence, often associated with the early settlements that served as religious and political centers in the region.

By the early Islamic period, *Staysa* was adopted by scholars and jurists to refer to the territory’s role as a beacon of piety and law in the Tayanusan world. Over time, the name evolved into Stasia, reflecting both linguistic modernization and national identity. The founding fathers of the republic chose the name to emphasize the country’s historic mission—to be a moral, sovereign, and progressive nation built on faith and civic order.

The title “State of Stasia” used in the constitution highlights this continuity: it honors the past while declaring the republic’s role as a modern Islamic state dedicated to freedom, God, and progress, in alignment with its national motto.

Geography

Stasia is located in eastern Tayanusa, bordered to the west by Cusea, to the south and east by the Blue Sea, and to the north by the Grandic Ocean. Its strategic coastal position between two major bodies of water gives it vital access to regional trade routes, fisheries, and shipping lanes, while also providing natural defense and maritime influence.

The country’s geography is primarily desert, with vast expanses of arid terrain dominating the central and eastern regions. These areas are characterized by rocky plateaus, sand basins, and seasonal dry wadis, forming a harsh but historically navigable landscape used by nomadic tribes and traders.

To the west, near the border with Cusea, lies the Al-Safin Plain, a wide and fertile region nourished by the country’s only permanent river, the Wadi Al-Hayat, which also forms the natural boundary with Cusea. This river valley supports agriculture, livestock farming, and serves as a vital water source for nearby towns and transport corridors.

The area surrounding Nova Stasia, the capital, is one of the most agriculturally productive zones in the country. Located on the edge of the desert and the plain, the capital benefits from underground aquifers, intensive irrigation, and proximity to coastal trade ports. The region hosts orchards, grain fields, and greenhouse farming, supplying food to much of the nation.

Stasia’s coastline stretches along both the Blue Sea and the Grandic Ocean, providing opportunities for fishing, port development, and tourism. Though sparsely populated, the northern coastal regions contain ecological reserves and areas of religious pilgrimage, accessible via improved road infrastructure.

Despite the arid nature of much of its land, Stasia’s varied terrain—from deserts and plains to coastal belts and river valleys—supports a mix of traditional rural livelihoods and emerging urban-industrial centers, contributing to the country’s resilience and regional role.

Politics

Government

Stasia is governed as a unitary presidential republic, combining centralized executive authority with a representative legislature and a religiously informed judicial system. The structure of the state is defined by the Constitution, which establishes the principles of national sovereignty, Islamic values, and civic order, while assigning clear powers and responsibilities to each branch of government.

The executive branch is the dominant force in Stasian politics. The President, elected by popular vote for a single renewable term, serves as both head of state and chief of the executive. The President holds broad constitutional authority, including the power to appoint ministers, issue decrees, oversee foreign relations, direct national defense, and manage internal security. The executive governs through a cabinet of ministers, each responsible for a specific policy domain such as defense, education, health, economy, and religious affairs.

Presidential authority is supported by the Deputy President, who functions as an institutional second-in-command and can represent the executive branch in legislative sessions, foreign visits, and national initiatives when delegated. The executive has exclusive authority to propose national budgets, emergency laws, and structural reforms, subject to legislative review.

The legislative branch consists of the unicameral National Assembly, whose members are elected every five years through a controlled and regulated electoral system. The Assembly has the constitutional power to debate and pass laws, ratify treaties, and supervise executive conduct through inquiries and motions. While functioning as a legislative body, the Assembly operates under certain ideological limits—namely, all proposed legislation must conform to Islamic principles, and no law may contradict the spiritual foundations of the state.

Parliamentary sessions are structured, with a presiding Speaker moderating debate and enforcing rules of procedure. Most legislation is introduced by the executive, though Assembly committees may draft their own proposals, especially in matters related to infrastructure, education, and social welfare. The Assembly may summon ministers for questioning, though formal votes of no confidence are rare and require constitutional thresholds.

The judiciary is structured around a dual legal system, with both civil and Sharia-based courts operating under the oversight of the Supreme Court. The judiciary is constitutionally independent, though judges are vetted for both legal expertise and religious integrity, ensuring alignment with the state’s moral and ideological framework. The highest court, led by a Chief Justice, has authority over constitutional interpretation, disputes between institutions, and appeals involving major criminal or administrative cases. Sharia courts handle matters of personal status, family law, inheritance, and religious conduct.

In essence, Stasia’s government is a centralized but religiously guided republican system, where executive power dominates, legislative participation exists within ideological limits, and legal authority is fused with faith-based norms. This model seeks to combine modern governance mechanisms with a strong religious and cultural foundation, maintaining stability and cohesion in a society deeply shaped by its historical and spiritual legacy.

Political parties

Political life in Stasia is formally organized around a multi-party framework, but in practice, it operates under a controlled political environment in which state-approved ideological conformity and religious alignment are mandatory. While elections are held regularly for the National Assembly, the political landscape is heavily dominated by a single ruling party, and opposition is tightly restricted and monitored.

The ruling force is the National Unity Movement, an Islamist-nationalist party that serves as the political engine of the executive branch. It promotes a platform based on Islamic governance, developmental statism, social conservatism, and military-backed order. The NUM holds an overwhelming majority of seats in the Assembly and controls all major policy initiatives, often functioning as an extension of the presidency rather than as an independent political entity.

Other political parties exist but must be licensed and reviewed by the Electoral Ethics Commission, which evaluates all platforms for their compatibility with national values, Islamic law, and state security interests. Most of these parties act as loyal opposition, advocating for limited reform, local issues, or technocratic governance, without challenging the religious or political status quo.

Parties are explicitly prohibited from promoting secularism, ethnic separatism, or ideologies considered foreign-influenced or subversive. Any group found to be spreading dissent, undermining Islamic morals, or encouraging anti-state sentiment can be dissolved by executive decree.

Campaigns are permitted but occur under strict limitations, including state-approved media access, monitoring of public speeches, and restrictions on foreign funding or civil society collaboration. Political debates are typically focused on administrative competence, regional development, or religious virtue, rather than open ideological contestation.

While the appearance of choice is maintained through electoral competition, the real power remains concentrated in a single-party-dominant system, where religion, state loyalty, and executive control define the boundaries of permissible political activity. In this model, political parties serve more as channels of managed participation than as autonomous actors, ensuring that national unity and Islamic principles remain unquestioned pillars of the republic.

Administrative subdivisions

Stasia is administratively structured as a unitary state divided into seven governorates, each functioning as an extension of the central government’s authority. These governorates are designed not as autonomous regions but as administrative zones, with limited local discretion and full subordination to national policies and priorities.

Each governorate is headed by a Governor (Wali), appointed directly by the President and confirmed by the Ministry of Internal Affairs. The Governor serves as the regional representative of the executive, responsible for enforcing national laws, overseeing public administration, coordinating with national security forces, and ensuring compliance with religious and civil codes.

Within the governorates, the territory is further divided into districts (mudiriyat) and municipalities (baladiyat), which manage local services, such as waste collection, civil records, education oversight, and infrastructure maintenance. While some local administrators are drawn from tribal or community elites, all appointments are ultimately subject to central approval and periodic review.

Each municipality is governed by a Mayor (Ra’is al-Balad), who is nominated by the Ministry of Interior and tasked with executing local development plans, organizing religious and civic events, and maintaining public order in collaboration with the national police.

The capital city, Nova Stasia, holds a special administrative status as Capital City, governed directly by the Presidential Office for Capital Affairs. It hosts all national ministries, courts, and foreign missions, and receives higher funding and infrastructure priority compared to other regions. It is also the focal point for national religious events, military parades, and ceremonial functions.

While the constitution permits localized administrative frameworks, there is no form of regional autonomy or legislative authority granted to governorates. All legal and policy decisions are made centrally, and governorates primarily serve to implement, monitor, and report on national directives.

Religious oversight is integrated into the administrative structure, with each governorate assigned a Regional Religious Council, working closely with local clerics, schools, and courts to ensure uniform application of Islamic law and moral standards across all local jurisdictions. In summary, Stasia’s administrative divisions reflect a system of high centralization, with strong vertical control, limited regional flexibility, and a governance model where national unity, religious discipline, and executive oversight remain paramount at every level.

Foreign relations

Stasia pursues a strategically cautious and ideologically disciplined foreign policy, centered on the principles of Islamic solidarity, non-interference, and the protection of national sovereignty. As a member of major international and regional bodies such as the United Nations, Tayanusan Community, Islamic Cooperation Organization, Global Reserve Fund, International Energy Agency, and the International Tribunal for Justice, Stasia maintains a visible diplomatic presence while carefully managing the scope of its external engagements.

The country’s foreign relations are strongly influenced by its Islamic identity and unitary nationalist ideology. Stasia consistently aligns itself with Tayanusan Muslim-majority states, particularly Azaria, Ricene, and Sifalam, with whom it shares religious doctrine, defense priorities, and cultural diplomacy. These relationships are reinforced through bilateral agreements on education, religious outreach, energy cooperation, and joint port development.

Stasia maintains cordial but limited ties with select Edrobean countries and Valmirica, especially those that refrain from promoting secularism or liberal interventionism. Trade relations are strictly transactional, focusing on infrastructure technology, pharmaceuticals, and energy services, while cultural or political cooperation is avoided unless aligned with religious and national values.

The country does not participate in any military alliances and prohibits foreign bases on its territory. It adheres to a doctrine of defensive neutrality, investing in domestic military development and regional security frameworks without engaging in external conflicts or coalitions. It supports peace mediation and Islamic dispute resolution mechanisms within the Tayanusan Community and occasionally hosts religious summits to promote unity among Sunni-majority nations.

Stasia takes a firm stance against foreign influence in domestic policy, maintaining strict controls on foreign NGOs, external funding, and cross-border media. Its diplomatic missions are primarily tasked with protecting religious students abroad, securing economic contracts, and representing the country’s position in religious and international legal forums.

The foreign ministry works closely with the Supreme Religious Council to ensure that all international treaties, memoranda, and bilateral agreements are compatible with Islamic jurisprudence, national morality, and constitutional integrity.

Military

Stasia maintains a compact but highly centralized military known as the Stasian National Defense Forces, tasked with ensuring territorial integrity, internal stability, and the defense of the Islamic constitutional order. The military is strictly defensive in doctrine and operates under the principle of strategic autonomy, avoiding foreign entanglements while maintaining high levels of domestic readiness.

The President serves as Commander-in-Chief, holding ultimate authority over all military decisions, including mobilization, appointments of senior officers, and defense strategy. Operational command is exercised through the Ministry of Defense, which oversees budgeting, procurement, military education, and coordination with civil authorities during national emergencies. The SNDF is composed of three core branches:

  • Ground Force – The largest and most active branch, responsible for border security, desert patrols, and infrastructure defense. Units are stationed near the Cusean border, across the Al-Safin Plain, and around critical urban and transport hubs. Ground forces are trained in desert warfare, rapid response, and riot control.
  • Navy – Operating along the Blue Sea and Grandic Ocean, this branch maintains patrol vessels, coastal surveillance stations, and anti-smuggling teams. While not a traditional navy, it serves to monitor maritime borders, protect shipping routes, and assist in humanitarian operations during floods or coastal crises.
  • Air Force – A smaller force equipped with light drones, fixed-wing aircraft, and helicopters, used for reconnaissance, logistics, and border monitoring. Its main base is near Nova Stasia, and it operates jointly with the intelligence service for national threat assessments.

Military service is mandatory for men aged 18 to 22, with a minimum term of 18 months. Exemptions are rare and require formal approval from the Ministry of Defense. Military education emphasizes discipline, religious ethics, and national unity, and is closely supervised by clerics assigned to each training base. The SNDF does not operate abroad and Stasia refuses participation in military alliances, including under regional frameworks. Its doctrine explicitly prohibits involvement in foreign wars, placing emphasis on sovereign defense, internal cohesion, and civil-military integration during times of national mobilization.

Military spending averages around 3.2% of GDP, focused on maintenance, training, and domestic defense technology, rather than foreign acquisitions or power projection. Joint military exercises with Tayanusan partners occasionally occur but are framed as defensive drills, not combat readiness tests. In Stasian national culture, the military is portrayed as the guardian of divine law and national order—a disciplined force that stands as a bulwark against foreign aggression, ideological subversion, and domestic unrest. Its presence reinforces the state’s broader commitment to Islamic governance, political sovereignty, and strategic independence.

Law enforcement

Law enforcement in Stasia is highly centralized, functioning as both a mechanism of public order and an instrument of religious and political discipline. The system is structured under the authority of the Ministry of Internal Affairs and Moral Security, which oversees all police operations, internal intelligence, and coordination with the judiciary.

The national police force is organized into regional directorates across the country’s governorates and municipalities. Officers are trained in criminal law, Sharia compliance, and public order enforcement, with a strong emphasis on ideological loyalty and religious conduct. Their duties range from routine policing—such as traffic control, theft prevention, and civil disputes—to the enforcement of moral codes related to modesty, religious practice, and public behavior.

A distinct branch known as the Morality Patrols operates alongside the main police force, tasked with ensuring compliance with Islamic norms. These units monitor dress codes, prayer attendance during work hours, Ramadan fasting, and the public behavior of youth and women. They work closely with local clerics and report to both the Ministry and regional Religious Oversight Councils. In urban centers and border zones, the police collaborate with specialized divisions such as:

  • Border and Customs Police, responsible for anti-smuggling operations and monitoring cross-border movement, especially along the Cusean frontier and the maritime checkpoints.
  • Cyber and Media Police, which monitors online content for anti-state rhetoric, religious deviation, or the promotion of secular or foreign ideologies.
  • Municipal Security Police, who handle crowd control, religious processions, and enforcement of curfews or restrictions during times of unrest or heightened alert.

The State Intelligence Directorate, operating in parallel with the police, is a powerful body responsible for surveillance, subversion detection, and political control. It monitors activist networks, clerical dissent, unauthorized gatherings, and foreign-linked NGOs, often using extensive informant networks. Arrests for sedition, ideological deviation, or blasphemy are typically carried out in cooperation between intelligence and police units. In national ideology, law enforcement is not seen merely as a security institution—it is an extension of the moral state, responsible for protecting faith, order, and national unity. Officers are expected to embody the ethical standards of Islamic citizenship and act as guardians of the republic’s moral and religious foundations.

Economy

Stasia's economy is a state-guided, religiously regulated system with a focus on resource management, agricultural development, and infrastructure modernization, guided by the principles of Islamic finance and national self-reliance. With a GDP of 370 billion DTR, it ranks 18th globally, and is considered a rising mid-tier economy within the Tayanusan Community.

The country’s economic foundation rests on three main pillars:

  • Stasia’s most productive region is the Al-Safin Plain and the fertile zones near Nova Stasia, supported by irrigation systems, greenhouse farming, and state-managed grain cooperatives. The nation is largely self-sufficient in staple foods such as wheat, barley, and dates, and is a regional exporter of citrus fruits and halal livestock products. Agricultural policy is closely tied to land reform initiatives and zakat-supported rural development programs.
  • While Stasia lacks major oil reserves, it possesses valuable phosphate deposits, construction-grade stone, and rare earth minerals, particularly in the eastern desert provinces. These resources are extracted and exported under state control, with foreign partnerships limited to joint ventures approved by the Ministry of Energy and Economic Stewardship. Revenues are funneled into the national budget and religious endowments.
  • Nova Stasia and other coastal cities serve as hubs for trade, religious tourism, and regional shipping. Pilgrimage centers and religious universities attract visitors from across Tayanusa, while port facilities on the Blue Sea support modest maritime commerce with Azaria and Ricene. Customs operations are strictly monitored and restricted to ideologically aligned partners.

Stasia's financial system is fully Sharia-compliant. Interest-based lending is forbidden, and all banks operate through Islamic contracts such as murabaha (cost-plus sales), ijara (leasing), and musharaka (profit-sharing). A national Zakat Fund is used to redistribute wealth, subsidize education and healthcare, and support family formation.

The economy is heavily public-sector dependent, with the government employing a majority of the workforce in ministries, infrastructure projects, and religious institutions. Unemployment among youth remains a policy concern, addressed through state-run training centers, apprenticeship programs, and military-linked employment schemes.

Foreign investment is limited and carefully regulated. Foreign companies may only operate through joint ventures with domestic entities approved by the government, and are prohibited from engaging in sectors deemed culturally or religiously sensitive. Economic ties are strongest with fellow Islamic nations, particularly in energy, food security, and religious infrastructure.

Trade is primarily bilateral, focused on Tayanusan neighbors. Exports include agricultural goods, phosphate, religious textiles, and construction materials; imports are mostly industrial machinery, medicine, and fuel.

In summary, Stasia’s economy is defined by its religious orientation, state control, and focus on self-sufficiency, seeking to balance moral governance with gradual modernization. It is not designed for rapid liberal growth, but for stability, national dignity, and the long-term sustainability of a faith-based republic.

Demography

Stasia has a population of approximately 4,736,452 people, making it a small but tightly governed nation within the Tayanusan region. The demographic profile is shaped by high levels of religious conformity, urban-rural integration, and a disciplined, youthful population that reflects the state’s focus on moral education, family structure, and national service.

The majority of the population is concentrated in and around the capital city Nova Stasia, the Al-Safin Plain, and coastal urban zones along the Blue Sea. These regions host the bulk of the country’s infrastructure, economic activity, and educational institutions. In contrast, the desert interior is sparsely populated, with small communities centered around military outposts, religious schools, and state-supported agricultural settlements.

The median age in Stasia is 28 years, indicating a relatively young population. This youthfulness is the result of stable fertility rates, traditional family structures, and early marriage policies promoted through state-run family initiatives. The government provides incentives for marriage and childbirth, including housing aid and subsidies tied to religious compliance.

Gender roles are clearly defined in accordance with Islamic values. Men dominate the public sphere—working in administration, military, education, and law enforcement—while women are primarily engaged in family life, religious teaching, healthcare, and gender-segregated educational roles. While female literacy is near-universal, women’s participation in political and legal authority is minimal, consistent with religious doctrine and constitutional custom.

Education is universal and state-managed, with a strong focus on religious studies, moral discipline, and patriotic instruction. Qur’anic memorization, national history, and civic obedience are central to the curriculum. Literacy rates are high, and vocational training is emphasized for both genders, especially in areas aligned with state employment needs.

Healthcare is free and centrally administered, particularly in areas such as maternal care, child vaccination, and rural health access. The system also incorporates Islamic medical ethics, with hospital chaplaincy, religious health education, and gender-segregated facilities standard across the country.

Stasia also hosts a small number of internal migrants from more rural or tribal regions, typically relocated by the government for employment, training, or ideological integration programs. Immigration is nearly non-existent, as the country strictly controls its borders and grants citizenship only under exceptional conditions of religious, cultural, and linguistic alignment.

Language

The official and only recognized language of Stasia is Tayanusan, which functions as the sole medium of government, education, media, legal proceedings, and religious instruction. Tayanusan is not just a tool of communication, but a core element of national identity, closely tied to the country’s religious, political, and cultural foundations.

Fluency in Tayanusan is mandatory for citizenship, public employment, and participation in civic life. All official documents, public signage, and school curricula are written exclusively in standardized Tayanusan, and no other languages are permitted in administrative or legal contexts. The Ministry of Religious and Cultural Affairs oversees language policy, script regulation, and linguistic conformity, ensuring that Tayanusan usage reflects both classical Islamic values and national ideology.

While regional dialects or tribal speech patterns may exist in informal settings, they are not acknowledged officially and are discouraged in public discourse. The use of foreign languages—particularly Edrobean or Western ones—is limited to technical institutes or diplomatic training centers, and is not promoted in the general education system. Language is also deeply integrated into religious education, with early schooling focused on Qur’anic grammar, memorization, and rhetorical eloquence. Students are expected to master both modern usage and classical forms to ensure a deep understanding of religious texts and legal doctrine.

Religion

Religion is the foundational pillar of public life, governance, and law in Stasia. The country is a formally Islamic republic, with Islam enshrined in the constitution as the source of all legislation, national identity, and civic duty. Approximately 90% of the population identifies as Muslim, predominantly Sunni, following the Tayanusan tradition of jurisprudence. The remaining 10%, largely composed of small Christian and tribal minority communities, are permitted to worship privately but hold no influence in public affairs.

Islam in Stasia is not only a personal faith but a political and legal authority. All laws must conform to Sharia, and no legislation may contradict the principles of the Qur’an or the rulings of the High Religious Council, which functions as both a theological body and a constitutional watchdog. The President, judges, and senior officials are required to be Muslims and to demonstrate public adherence to Islamic practice.

The Ministry of Religious Affairs oversees every aspect of Islamic life, including the administration of mosques, religious schools, clerical appointments, sermon content, and the publication of religious materials. All imams must be certified by state institutions, and Friday sermons are standardized nationwide to promote unity and ideological discipline.

Religious education is mandatory at all levels of schooling. From an early age, students are taught Qur’anic memorization, classical Arabic grammar, Islamic ethics, and the legal history of Islamic governance. Advanced students may enter religious academies, which produce clerics, judges, and teachers aligned with the republic’s doctrinal views.

Public life is structured around Islamic rituals. State ceremonies begin with prayer, workplaces are required to accommodate the five daily prayers, and all public institutions close during Ramadan, Eid al-Fitr, and Eid al-Adha. Zakat (obligatory almsgiving) is collected through a centralized fund and redistributed for social welfare, education, and religious infrastructure.

Minority faiths may worship in designated buildings under strict licensing but may not proselytize, build new structures without approval, or hold public religious events. Conversion from Islam is illegal and punishable, and blasphemy or criticism of Islamic institutions is treated as a national security offense.

Ethnicities

Stasia is an ethnically homogeneous nation, with Stasians making up approximately 90% of the population. The Stasians are a culturally unified people who speak Tayanusan natively and share a deep historical and religious identity rooted in the region’s Islamic heritage. They form the political, social, and religious majority, and define the national character of the republic.

The remaining 10% of the population consists of minority groups, including small tribal communities, coastal dwellers of mixed ancestry, and a limited number of Christian minorities concentrated in a few urban neighborhoods near the Blue Sea. While these groups retain some elements of distinct cultural identity in private, public life is expected to conform to the linguistic, religious, and ideological norms of the state.

Ethnicity is not recognized as a legal tag in Stasia. The government promotes a strict policy of national assimilation, emphasizing that all citizens are first and foremost Muslim Tayanusan-speaking Stasians, regardless of ancestral background. Public expressions of ethnic distinction—such as cultural festivals, tribal political movements, or linguistic variation—are discouraged, and in some cases, suppressed under the banner of national unity.

The state’s ideological model prioritizes religious and linguistic homogeneity over multiculturalism, with all education, legal procedures, and media content aligned to a single national narrative. Minorities are expected to adopt the dominant norms in dress, speech, and public behavior, and are generally excluded from positions of political or religious influence unless fully assimilated.

In practice, this has produced a highly cohesive and disciplined society, but at the cost of ethnic invisibility for minority groups. The government considers this a necessary condition for maintaining order, faith, and ideological clarity within the framework of its Islamic national republic.
