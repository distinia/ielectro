The Tayanusan League is a regional intergovernmental organization composed of sovereign states located on the continent of Tayanusa. The TC seeks to promote economic cooperation, regional connectivity, political coordination, and cultural unity across Tayanusa, regardless of the form of government, religious alignment, or internal legal systems of its members.

Founded on the principles of non-interference, equal sovereignty, and strategic self-reliance, the TC represents a unique model of regional integration that prioritizes continental solidarity without demanding political uniformity. It comprises a diverse range of states—from monarchies to democracies—and operates on the basis of consensus and pragmatic diplomacy rather than supranational authority.

The Union functions through a three-tier institutional framework, consisting of the Political Committee, the Tayanusan Assembly, and the General Secretariat, headquartered in Meiyah. These bodies coordinate policy in areas such as trade, infrastructure, energy, education, defense cooperation, and regional development. Unlike other international unions, the TC emphasizes inter-governmental coordination over legal harmonization, allowing member states to preserve their political and cultural systems while working toward common goals.

Economically, the TC supports continental trade corridors, energy interdependence, and state-led industrial development, with key infrastructure projects linking ports, inland cities, and extractive regions. A shared focus on transport integration, customs harmonization, and supply chain resilience has increased intra-Tayanusan commerce and reduced dependency on foreign actors. While the majority of member economies are governed by Islamic finance principles, the TC itself is religiously neutral, welcoming secular cooperation.

Culturally, the Union fosters Pan-Tayanusan identity, emphasizing a shared linguistic heritage in the Tayanusan language, mutual respect for diverse ethnic traditions, and joint celebration of artistic and sporting events. Institutions under the Commissioner for Culture and Identity support cross-border cultural initiatives, youth exchanges, and preservation of oral histories and continental narratives.

The TC maintains observer status relationships with select Islamic and Global South countries, and engages in strategic partnerships with blocs such as the Edrobean Union, the United Nations, and the Global Reserve Fund. It advocates a multipolar global order and increased autonomy for regional powers.

While some public voices and intellectuals have called for eventual political unification of Tayanusa into a single continental state, most leaders and citizens support the current pluralistic and pragmatic structure of the Union. The TC is thus widely seen as a successful embodiment of unity through diversity—a platform where Tayanusa governs itself, by its own values, in pursuit of shared destiny, sovereignty, and regional renaissance.

Etymology

The name Tayanusa is believed to originate from the proto-Tayanusan root "Tay-an-ūshā", which scholars interpret as meaning “land of faith and breath” or “sacred expanse of life.” This term first appeared in early scriptural poetry and oral epics passed down by nomadic priest-scribes of the southern highlands, who described Tayanusa as a divine realm granted to humankind for spiritual discipline and communal survival. Over centuries, the term became geographically associated with the vast stretch of land between the Grandic Ocean and the Blue Sea, where early civilizations emerged around seasonal rivers and sacred hills.

As written culture developed, particularly under the influence of classical clerical dynasties in eastern Tayanusa, the name was gradually standardized into Tayanusa, combining “Tay”, meaning “life-giver” or “source,” with “Anusa”, a composite referring to divine space or entrusted territory. Religious texts from this period increasingly referenced Tayanusa not merely as a physical place, but as a civilizational mission, a continent divinely charged with the burden and glory of balancing sovereignty with obedience, law with mercy.

Politics

Institutions

The institutions of the Tayanusan League are structured to reflect a balance between continental coordination and state sovereignty, grounded in the Union’s guiding principles of religious solidarity, non-interference, and shared strategic development. They are not designed to mimic federal or supranational structures, but rather to provide a unified framework for consultation, cooperation, and joint action, particularly in matters of defense, infrastructure, energy, and cultural preservation.

At the heart of the institutional system is a clear hierarchy of consensus-building, where decisions are made through negotiation rather than imposition, and where each member state, regardless of size or system of governance, retains equal standing and equal voice.

The Political Committee serves as the chief strategic body of the Union. Composed of the heads of state or government of all member countries, it convenes biannually to determine the political direction, regional priorities, and external posture of the Union. It functions on the principle of non-binding consensus, meaning that while no decisions are enforceable over national sovereignty, agreed-upon measures carry strong political weight and are typically implemented across the bloc. The Political Committee also has the authority to issue joint declarations, coordinate defensive postures, and recommend new initiatives to the Assembly and Secretariat.

The Secretariat of the Tayanusan League, based in Tarek, Lamberia, is the administrative and diplomatic engine of the organization. Headed by a Secretary General, appointed every five years by consensus of the Political Committee, the Secretariat ensures the day-to-day coordination of Union initiatives, organizes summits, maintains communication with member governments, and oversees relations with observer states and international partners. The Secretariat also manages special agencies under its purview, such as the Tayanusan Energy Commission, the Council for Strategic Infrastructure, and the Tayanusan Office for Cultural Affairs. The Secretary General does not act as a political leader but as a facilitator and administrator, ensuring institutional continuity and operational coherence.

The Tayanusan Assembly is the deliberative organ of the Union. Each member state appoints an equal number of delegates, usually drawn from national parliaments, senior religious authorities, or academic institutions. The Assembly meets twice a year to debate resolutions on matters of economic policy, cultural exchange, transport connectivity, and defense cooperation. Though its decisions are not legally binding, Assembly Resolutions carry significant moral and political authority, especially when passed by consensus. The Assembly also provides a forum for grievance airing, proposal submission, and continental dialogue, making it a key space for managing intra-regional tensions and advancing shared values.

While the Tayanusan League does not operate a judicial or enforcement arm, it encourages alignment of national laws on cross-border trade, security coordination, and religious standards, and supports harmonization through model legislation and technical assistance. It is through these institutions—the Political Committee, the Secretariat, and the Assembly—that the Tayanusan League operates as a civilizational union of Islamic and national sovereignties, devoted to defending the continent’s religious unity, strategic autonomy, and moral order.

Member States

Membership in the Tayanusan League is open to all sovereign states geographically located within the continent of Tayanusa, regardless of their system of government, internal political ideology, or religious structure. The Union explicitly embraces the principle of continental inclusivity, recognizing that the path to regional unity does not require uniformity in governance. Whether a member state is a presidential republic, parliamentary monarchy, federal state, military regime, or theocratic monarchy, the Tayanusan League does not impose a normative framework or ideological litmus test for admission. Instead, its foundation rests on mutual respect, non-interference, and the pursuit of shared continental interests.

To become a full member, a country must meet only two core criteria: it must be geographically situated in Tayanusa, and it must commit to the principles outlined in the Foundational Charter, including respect for sovereignty, non-aggression against fellow members, and cooperation in designated areas such as infrastructure, trade, and regional security. There is no requirement to be secular, nor is there a preference for religious governance; both Islamic republics and secular states coexist equally under the Union framework. This reflects a deep understanding that continental unity must be based on shared purpose, not shared ideology.

Admission procedures begin with the submission of a formal request to the Political Committee, which then evaluates the applicant’s territorial status and readiness to uphold the obligations of membership. Once endorsed by a qualified majority of existing members, the applicant is invited to sign the Treaty of Tayanusan Cooperation, thereby gaining full access to the Union’s rights, institutions, and programs. Upon ratification, the new member immediately acquires representation in the Assembly, participation in Union summits, and eligibility for regional funding, infrastructure coordination, and joint defense planning.

Member states retain absolute sovereignty over their internal affairs. The Union does not legislate within domestic borders, nor does it intervene in political disputes, religious structures, or constitutional arrangements. This position has enabled cooperation between governments with vastly different models—from absolute monarchies to democratic republics, from Islamic states to pragmatic secular regimes. What binds them together is not ideological conformity, but geopolitical identity and strategic alignment as Tayanusans.

The observer status within the Tayanusan League is reserved exclusively for Islamic countries outside the continent of Tayanusa. This special provision reflects the Union’s ideological foundations in religious fraternity and civilizational continuity, particularly with the broader Islamic world. Observer states may attend summits, participate in religious and cultural dialogues, and contribute to infrastructure and development forums, but they do not possess voting rights, nor do they influence core decision-making processes. The observer designation is symbolic of religious solidarity, without diluting the geographical integrity of Tayanusan governance.

Observer status is not granted to secular non-Tayanusan states, regardless of their economic or strategic importance. This policy ensures that the Tayanusan League remains distinctly regional in identity, while also fostering selective transcontinental cooperation with countries that share its religious and cultural values. Such a structure enables the Union to build pan-Islamic partnerships while preserving its continental sovereignty and institutional autonomy.

Foreign relations

Foreign relations within the Tayanusan League are guided by a doctrine of continental solidarity, religious fraternity, and geopolitical autonomy, rooted in a shared vision of a strong, sovereign, and self-reliant Tayanusa. The Union rejects all forms of dependency on extra-continental power blocs and explicitly refuses alignment with global hegemonies, whether ideological, economic, or military. Instead, it pursues a non-aligned, multipolar foreign policy, placing Tayanusan interests, Islamic unity, and regional stability at the center of its diplomatic posture.

The Union does not speak with a single voice in all matters of global diplomacy—each member retains full sovereignty over its foreign affairs. However, on issues of continental concern, such as resource sovereignty, trade access, religious rights, and regional security, the Union operates through coordinated positions, adopted collectively through its Political Committee and Assembly resolutions. In these areas, member states frequently engage in joint declarations, economic countermeasures, and aligned voting strategies in international organizations, especially within the United Nations and its affiliated bodies.

Relations with Islamic countries outside Tayanusa are prioritized through observer status, religious exchange, and joint development initiatives. The Union maintains particularly strong ties with Comussania, Destenia, and selected Edrobean states, but always on the basis of mutual respect and non-interference. While it welcomes strategic partnerships with the Global South, it categorically rejects any conditional aid, structural adjustment mandates, or ideological exportation from Edrobean liberal democracies or Litherian technocracies.

In its approach to international aid and humanitarian diplomacy, the Tayanusan League insists on sovereignty-first principles. It provides assistance to fellow Islamic nations and disaster-stricken regions, but refuses participation in military peacekeeping missions under foreign command. The Union upholds the right of resistance against occupation, the defense of Islamic sanctities, and the non-negotiable status of religious and cultural identity in global governance frameworks.

The Tayanusan League is open to infrastructure diplomacy, trade corridors, and regional summits with non-Islamic entities—but only when such cooperation respects its religious and ideological boundaries. Cultural imperialism, NGO penetration, and ideological conditionalities are strictly forbidden in all foreign agreements. The Union promotes a Tayanusan worldview, where unity, honor, and divine order take precedence over globalist homogenization or transactional diplomacy.

Military

The military structure of the Tayanusan League is built upon the principles of sovereign defense, continental deterrence, and religious solidarity, without infringing upon the independence of its member states. The TC does not maintain a standing army, nor does it possess any centralized military command capable of overriding national authority. Instead, it functions as a framework for coordination, interoperability, and strategic consultation among the armed forces of its members—who retain full control over their own defense policies, military doctrines, and force deployment.

The central military body of the TC is the Tayanusan Defense Coordination Council, composed of the defense ministers and chiefs of staff from all member states. The TDCC convenes at least once annually and in emergency sessions when required. It is responsible for joint planning, intelligence exchange, military-industrial cooperation, and the supervision of continental military exercises. The Council may issue military recommendations, agree on shared procurement standards, and create interoperability protocols, but all implementation is strictly voluntary and nationally executed.

In line with the TC’s vision of pan-Islamic sovereignty and continental resilience, the member states have agreed on a mutual defense understanding, stating that any act of aggression against a TC member will be considered a threat to the whole Union. However, the nature and extent of each state's response is left to national discretion, ensuring that no state is compelled to intervene beyond its constitutional or religious constraints.

The TC actively encourages joint military exercises, especially in border defense, anti-smuggling operations, desert warfare, and cyber defense, often hosted by the largest regional militaries—such as those of Cusea, Lamberia, and Sifalam. These drills focus on strengthening interoperability, building trust among officers, and promoting a unified military culture rooted in Islamic ethics and continental loyalty.

Additionally, the Union fosters collaboration in defense technology, promoting the joint development of arms, drone systems, and communication infrastructure through the Tayanusan Armaments and Innovation Network, a voluntary association of defense industries and research institutes from participating countries. This has resulted in growing military-industrial synergy between members such as Azaria, Suklan, and Ricene.

The TC forbids the establishment of foreign military bases on the soil of its members and rejects participation in external military alliances, upholding a strict doctrine of non-alignment and internal security sovereignty. Observer states may attend military summits and drills but are excluded from decision-making.

Law enforcement

Law enforcement in the Tayanusan League is structured around the principles of internal sovereignty, religious order, and regional cooperation, while fully respecting the independence of national policing and intelligence systems. The TC does not possess a supranational police force. Instead, it functions as a continental platform for coordination, information exchange, and harmonization of law enforcement practices, especially in areas of border security, terrorism prevention, narcotics trafficking, and moral order enforcement.

At the core of this structure is the Tayanusan Security and Justice Council, composed of the interior ministers and chief judicial officers of each member state. The Council oversees a network of working groups and liaison offices that facilitate:

  • Mutual recognition of warrants and fast-track extradition mechanisms
  • Joint surveillance and pursuit protocols for transborder criminal activity
  • Real-time communication channels between national intelligence agencies
  • Collective watchlists for suspects in terrorism, subversion, or anti-Islamic agitation

Member states are encouraged to align their criminal codes and security legislation, particularly in regard to offenses like blasphemy, subversion of the Islamic order, organized trafficking, and cross-border smuggling. However, each country retains its own definitions of criminality, due process, and penalties, and no TC organ may override national law.

The TC promotes theocratic-legal cooperation, with the TSJC supporting the development of Sharia-compatible penal codes, Islamic criminal procedure manuals, and ethics training for judges and police officers. The goal is to reinforce the religious and cultural cohesion of law enforcement across the continent, while respecting the diversity of Islamic jurisprudence practiced by each member. A key operational body is the Tayanusan Bureau for Internal Security Cooperation, headquartered in a neutral administrative city. It functions as an encrypted clearinghouse for:

  • Intelligence reports on extremist networks and ideological threats
  • Cross-referenced biometric and identity data systems
  • Analyses of emerging threats, including digital radicalization, narcotics flows, and border infiltration

In frontier regions and high-mobility zones, joint patrols between neighboring states are conducted, especially along the Cusean-Stasian corridor, Ricene-Toplan maritime routes, and desert frontiers of Sifalam and Azaria. These operations often combine regular police, religious patrols, and local tribal auxiliaries, coordinated via bilateral security protocols.

The TC has no uniform standard for civil liberties, due to the diversity of governance models among members. Some states, like Cusea and Sifalam, enforce rigid moral and religious policing, while others, like Stasia and Ricene, apply a blend of Sharia and administrative law with different levels of public enforcement. The TSJC respects this variation, provided member states uphold public order, Islamic values, and the sovereignty of the state.

The Union prohibits the operation of foreign-funded NGOs involved in law, justice reform, or ideological reeducation within its space, citing moral sovereignty and protection from liberal infiltration. International cooperation on law enforcement occurs only through TC-sanctioned bilateral channels, and all foreign observers are subject to cultural and religious vetting.

Economy

The economy of the Tayanusan League is founded on a model of continental self-reliance, Islamic economic principles, and mutual development through strategic sovereignty. While each member state retains full control over its national economy, the TC functions as a platform for economic coordination, resource pooling, and the creation of a unified economic identity distinct from Edrobean and Western financial models.

At the heart of the TC’s philosophy lies the belief that economic independence is inseparable from political sovereignty and religious order. Thus, the TC promotes policies rooted in zakat redistribution, interest-free finance, and the Islamic prohibition of speculative capital. While some members pursue state-driven development (such as Cusea, Sifalam, and Ricene), others like Lamberia combine Islamic frameworks with controlled market openness, demonstrating the flexible application of shared principles.

The TC does not seek to create a unified currency or supranational central bank. Instead, it encourages monetary coordination, with members agreeing to peg national currencies to a Tayanusan Dinar Reference benchmark—used only for accounting, trade settlements, and infrastructure financing. This system reduces reliance on volatile foreign currencies and increases internal trade transparency. Trade integration is a core objective. The Tayanusan Economic Cooperation Pact guarantees:

  • No tariffs or quantitative limits on intra-TC trade of strategic goods: energy, food, minerals, industrial machinery, and religious products
  • Free movement of Tayanusan workers and professionals, regulated by bilateral quotas and ideological vetting
  • Joint investment corridors across maritime and desert routes, particularly connecting Cusea, Toplan, Sifalam, and Lamberia
  • Protection of national strategic industries from foreign ownership, enforced through TC guidelines that limit Edrobean and external corporate penetration

Zakat funds and sovereign religious treasuries are being used more actively at the inter-state level, with member governments agreeing to allocate portions of their national zakat to TC Development Projects, including:

  • Desert highway systems and inland ports
  • Halal-certified agro-industrial hubs
  • Military-industrial complexes and dual-use logistics centers
  • Digital infrastructure, especially in Islamic e-learning, court archives, and internal messaging systems

Foreign investment in the TC is highly restricted. Only Islamic-majority observer states may apply for privileged access, and even then, only through joint ventures with TC-based public or religious institutions. All investment is subject to ideological screening, ensuring that no external project undermines faith, sovereignty, or moral economy. The TC’s economic philosophy is unapologetically non-liberal. It openly rejects globalist financial models and Edrobean austerity frameworks, favoring a productive Islamic economy based on state planning, religious authority, and technological sovereignty.

Demography

The Tayanusan League is home to a population of over 260 million people, making it one of the most populous regional blocs in the Global South. Despite the diversity of governance models, ethnic compositions, and economic structures among its six member states, the TC is marked by a shared demographic trajectory: youthful populations, rapid urbanization, and strong adherence to religious and cultural norms.

The most populous member is Cusea, with over 78 million inhabitants, followed by Lamberia (53 million), Azaria (49 million), Ricene (42 million), Sifalam (32 million), and Stasia (4.7 million). This demographic distribution reflects a geopolitical balance between continental powers like Cusea and Lamberia, mid-sized religious monarchies like Azaria and Sifalam, and smaller republics like Stasia.

The median age across the Union ranges between 26 and 31 years, indicating a predominantly youthful population. Fertility rates remain above replacement in all member states, supported by state-sponsored family policies, early marriage customs, and religious encouragement of large households. This youth bulge is considered both a developmental opportunity and a strategic imperative, driving investments in education, vocational training, and employment programs.

Urbanization is advancing rapidly, with major cities such as Virindal, Tarek, Azar, and Meiyah emerging as administrative, industrial, and religious centers. Still, rural populations remain significant, especially in agriculturally active regions like Toplan (Ricene), the Qalhad High Plains (Sifalam), and the Al-Safin Plain (Stasia). National policies in all member states emphasize controlled urban growth, rural integration, and housing development.

Population

While the peoples of the Tayanusan League are often referred to collectively as Tayanusan, they are not a single ethnic group but rather a civilizational family united by language, religion, and regional heritage. Each member state is home to distinct Tayanusan ethnicities, shaped by unique historical trajectories, tribal legacies, and cultural patterns.

For instance, Azaris, Cuseans, Lamberians, Sifalamis, Ricenese, and Stasians all share the Tayanusan language and Islamic faith, but differ in dialect, customs, tribal configurations, and ethnogenesis. This internal diversity is managed not through multiculturalism, but through religious and linguistic cohesion, with most states promoting a single national identity rooted in Islam and the Tayanusan tongue.

The total population of the TC surpasses 260 million, with the largest shares residing in Cusea (78 million), Lamberia (53 million), and Azaria (49 million). Smaller but strategic contributors include Ricene (42 million), Sifalam (32 million), and Stasia (4.7 million). Population density varies widely, from the coastal urban clusters of Ricene and Lamberia to the desert heartlands of Azaria and Sifalam.

Fertility rates across the Union remain above replacement level, with most countries averaging between 2.4 and 3.1 children per woman. This demographic resilience is attributed to religious encouragement of family life, early marriage customs, and pro-natalist welfare policies, including subsidized housing, zakat-based childcare, and marital grants. Although urbanization and education are beginning to slow birthrates in some regions (notably Nova Stasia and Azar), the overall trajectory remains one of continued population growth.

Education levels have improved significantly across the bloc over the last two decades. Literacy rates exceed 90% in all member states, with near-universal primary and secondary education enrollment. However, disparities persist: urban elites in Lamberia, Ricene, and Azaria often access high-quality technical and religious schooling, while rural populations in Stasia and inland Sifalam rely on state-run Islamic schools with limited resources. Tertiary education is expanding, but access remains uneven, with women’s participation restricted in more conservative states.

A growing concern for the TC is emigration, especially among the rural and underemployed youth. As economic inequality persists and social mobility remains constrained in certain provinces, many Tayanusan citizens seek better opportunities abroad. Over the last decade, Edrobe has become the primary destination for Tayanusan migrants, particularly Destenia, which absorbs an estimated 70% of all Tayanusan emigrants. Migrants are drawn by Destenia’s industrial economy, higher wages, and perceived social freedoms. This trend has triggered policy anxiety in the TC’s southern states, where emigration is seen as both a loss of human capital and a potential ideological vulnerability.

Despite this, the governments of the TC have not criminalized emigration. Instead, many are investing in youth employment schemes, rural industrialization, and religiously-aligned civic training to retain talent and reinforce national identity. The long-term demographic challenge for the TC is thus twofold: maintaining high fertility and religious cohesion at home, while managing the social consequences of outward migration in an increasingly interconnected global system.

Language

The Tayanusan language is the sole official and unifying language of the Tayanusan League. Spoken natively by the overwhelming majority of citizens in all member states, it serves as the exclusive medium of government, education, religion, media, and public administration. In the TC, language is not a neutral instrument — it is a sacred pillar of civilization, inseparable from Islamic jurisprudence, moral instruction, and political identity.

Though all members speak Tayanusan, each nation possesses its own dialectical flavor and lexical nuances, shaped by tribal heritage, geographic variation, and religious expression. For example, Azarian Tayanusan is marked by Quranic eloquence and classical phrasing, while Lamberian Tayanusan incorporates a faster, clipped syntax and administrative precision. These differences, however, remain entirely mutually intelligible, and are seen as ornaments of national character rather than divisions.

In every member state, fluency in Tayanusan is required for citizenship, civil service, military command, and religious authority. All schools operate exclusively in Tayanusan, with classical grammar, Qur’anic vocabulary, and rhetorical clarity forming the basis of early education. The Ministries of Religious and Cultural Affairs in each country are tasked with maintaining orthographic purity, reviewing school textbooks, and supervising public messaging to prevent linguistic degradation or foreign contamination.

Minority languages — including Racabi, tribal dialects, or coastal tongues — are tolerated only as private or poetic expressions. They are not recognized in law, not used in administration, and not permitted in official education. Public life is strictly monolingual, reflecting the Union’s belief that linguistic unity is a condition for moral unity. Efforts to preserve minority speech are allowed only in the form of oral tradition or religious storytelling, and only if they do not threaten Tayanusan primacy.

Foreign languages are rarely taught. While some technical institutes in Lamberia and Ricene offer basic instruction in Edrobean languages (especially Destenian) for trade and diplomacy, such programs are limited in scope and closely monitored. Edrobean languages, particularly those associated with liberal ideologies, are treated with caution or outright hostility, as potential vectors of cultural erosion and spiritual deviation.

Religion

Islam is the foundation and soul of the Tayanusan League. All member states officially declare Islam as the state religion, and all institutions — political, legal, educational, and cultural — are bound to uphold its authority. The Union does not treat religion as a private matter or symbolic identity: it is the essence of national unity, moral order, and continental purpose.

Sunni Islam, particularly the Tayanusan school of jurisprudence, is followed across the Union. While minor theological differences exist between states — with some practicing tribal customs or legal variations — all adhere to the five pillars, enforce public prayer, and organize society under Sharia. The religious elite, including the High Councils of Ulama and appointed jurists, serve as both spiritual guardians and constitutional advisors, ensuring that no law contradicts Islamic doctrine. Public life in all TC countries revolves around religious discipline:

  • Prayer times structure the workday, with government institutions pausing for collective worship.
  • Ramadan is universally observed, with state enforcement of fasting in public.
  • Zakat is mandatory and collected through government channels.
  • Blasphemy, apostasy, and religious insult are criminal offenses, prosecuted under national security or moral protection codes.

Religious education is mandatory at all levels. Qur’anic memorization, Islamic ethics, and Sharia studies are taught from the earliest schooling years, and religious instructors are trained, appointed, and certified by the state. No foreign doctrine, sectarian ideology, or secular philosophy is permitted in the national curriculum. The state views theological deviation as a threat to national cohesion.

Each state maintains a Ministry of Religious Affairs that controls all mosques, regulates sermon content, and supervises the appointment of imams. Friday sermons are often standardized, written by national authorities to emphasize themes such as loyalty, discipline, modesty, obedience to the state, and moral order.

While small Christian minorities exist in countries like Azaria and Ricene, they are permitted only private worship in licensed churches under strict supervision. Public religious expression by non-Muslims is not allowed, and proselytism is criminalized. The TC grants observer status only to other Islamic states and does not recognize religious pluralism within its political space.

In foreign policy, religion shapes diplomacy. The TC promotes Islamic solidarity in global forums, supports Islamic education abroad, funds mosques and Qur’anic institutes in friendly countries, and offers mediation in conflicts between Muslim nations. It rejects foreign ideological influence, particularly from secular or liberal systems, viewing them as incompatible with the divine moral order.

Culture

Sport

In the Tayanusan League, sport is not mere entertainment — it is national training, youth discipline, and a moral expression of unity. All member states view athletic development as a tool for strengthening the body in service of the faith, sharpening the mind for duty to the state, and fostering inter-state pride without division.

Football (soccer) is the dominant sport across Tayanusa, with national leagues in each country operating under strict codes of ethics, modesty, and national symbolism. Players are state role models, expected to reflect Islamic values, discipline, and respect for authority both on and off the field. Celebrations that violate moral conduct — such as arrogance, dancing, or profanity — are banned. Many players undergo mandatory religious training as part of their contracts.

The Tayanusan Cup, held every four years, is the most prestigious regional competition, drawing national teams from all six members of the TC. It is not merely a sporting event, but a continental celebration of unity through strength. Opening ceremonies begin with collective prayer, royal or presidential addresses, and Qur’anic recitations, followed by martial displays and the sporting tournament. The Tayanusan Cup is broadcast across the Islamic world, presented as a model of pious and disciplined competition.

The Ministries of Sport in each state oversee athlete conduct, facility management, and curriculum integration. Physical education is mandatory in schools and military academies, always accompanied by Qur’anic lessons on self-control, humility, and strength of will. In some states like Lamberia and Azaria, elite athletes must complete national service or religious instruction before representing the country abroad.

Public perception

Public perception of the Tayanusan League is overwhelmingly positive across the member states. Citizens widely regard the Union as a symbol of Islamic unity, cultural pride, and collective strength in an increasingly fragmented world. The TC is credited with restoring continental dignity, promoting inter-Islamic cooperation, and providing an effective alternative to secular international blocs.

Pan-Tayanusanism, the ideological foundation of the Union, enjoys strong support, particularly among youth, scholars, religious leaders, and civil society. The TC is seen as a vehicle for preserving Islamic values in governance and society, while also delivering regional development, infrastructure investment, and diplomatic leverage. Surveys and media across Azaria, Lamberia, Cusea, and Ricene regularly highlight high levels of public trust in TC institutions and a shared belief in its divine and historical mission.

Support for deeper integration is growing. Many advocate for the transformation of the Tayanusan League into a single continental state, citing religious commonality, a shared language (Tayanusan), and aligned geopolitical interests. However, proposals for full unification face certain cultural and structural limitations.

Ethnic distinctions, such as between Azaris, Sifalamis, and Toplanese, remain socially significant. While not politically divisive, they shape differing regional customs, dialects, and tribal traditions that complicate national homogenization. Additionally, political system diversity—ranging from absolute monarchies to parliamentary republics and federal Islamic republics — raises practical questions about what unified model of governance could apply. While some call for a hybrid Islamic system combining monarchy, Shura, and elected representation, no consensus yet exists.

Nonetheless, the Pan-Tayanusan identity has taken deep root in the public consciousness. Shared military drills, sporting events, religious campaigns, and infrastructure projects have fostered a sense of belonging that transcends borders. Many citizens speak of fellow Tayanusans as “brothers in faith and destiny.” Pilgrimages, student exchanges, and joint economic zones have further normalized continental interaction as a part of daily life.
