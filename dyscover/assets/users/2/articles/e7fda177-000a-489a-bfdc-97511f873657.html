The Union of Ricene and Toplan, commonly referred to as Ricene, is a federal presidential republic located in northern Tayanusa, bordered by Lamberia to the west, the Sea of Ricene to the south, and the Grandic Ocean to the north and east. Formed through the unification of the mainland region of Ricene and the island of Toplan, it covers an area of 275,328 km² and has a population of approximately 42.4 million. The capital city is Meiyah, situated along the southern coastline.

The country is governed as a federal Islamic state, combining strong presidential authority with religious oversight and limited regional autonomy. The President, currently Youssef El Amrani, serves as both head of state and commander-in-chief, while legislative authority is vested in the Federal Assembly, comprising the Council of Deputies and the Council of Regions. The judiciary is guided by Sharia law and overseen by the Federal Constitutional Court, led by the Chief Justice.

Ricene’s economy, valued at 1.0 trillion DTR, ranks 7th globally and is driven by natural gas exports, solar energy development, agriculture in Toplan, and large-scale infrastructure projects. The country follows Islamic economic principles, including zakat, interest-free banking, and strong state intervention in key industries such as energy, transport, and telecommunications.

The population is predominantly Muslim (97%), with a small Christian minority. Tayanusan is the official and only recognized language. Ethnically, the population is composed of 58% Ricenese, 36% Toplanese, and 4% others. Though culturally distinct, all groups are unified under the state’s model of religious-national identity. Ethnic and linguistic plurality is discouraged in favor of religious and ideological conformity.

The country’s internal security and law enforcement are managed through a centralized police force and a powerful intelligence agency. Public conduct is regulated in line with religious norms, and dissent against the federal structure or Islamic order is tightly suppressed. Military service is mandatory, and the Federal Armed Forces of Ricene focus on territorial defense and internal stability.

Ricene is a member of the United Nations, Islamic Cooperation Organization, Tayanusan Community, Global Reserve Fund, International Energy Agency, and other multilateral organizations. Its foreign policy emphasizes Islamic solidarity, non-alignment, and strategic economic cooperation, especially with Azaria, Sifalam, and select Edrobean states.

As one of the largest and most economically influential countries in Tayanusa, Ricene represents a fusion of religious governance, federal structure, and resource-led development, with a strong emphasis on national unity, ideological control, and regional leadership.

Etymology

The name Union of Ricene and Toplan reflects the political and geographic unification of two historically distinct regions—Ricene, a coastal and mercantile territory, and Toplan, an inland highland region known for its tribal confederations and strategic passes. The union was formalized in 2020 following decades of interregional cooperation, shared religious ties, and economic integration efforts.

The term Ricene is believed to originate from the ancient Tayanusan root *"Riqn"*, meaning “shore” or “coastline,” referencing the region’s position along the northern Ricene Sea and its long history as a center of maritime trade and religious scholarship.

Toplan, on the other hand, comes from a highland dialect word meaning “summit” or “upland land,” referring to the mountainous terrain and plateau systems that dominate the inland areas. It has historically been associated with fortified villages, tribal governance, and pastoral economies.

The modern state’s founders deliberately chose the dual name to emphasize equal recognition, mutual respect, and national unity between the two peoples. The shortened form, Ricene, is commonly used in international contexts, while the full title is preserved in legal and constitutional documents to symbolize the federal partnership and shared sovereignty that define the Union’s identity.

Geography

The Union of Ricene and Toplan spans a geographically diverse territory along the northeastern edge of the Tayanusan continent, bordered to the west by Lamberia, to the south by the Sea of Ricene, and to the north and east by the Grandic Ocean. This strategic location gives the country both continental and maritime advantages, making it a vital hub for shipping, energy transit, and cross-regional diplomacy. The Union is composed of two primary regions:

  • Ricene, the mainland portion, is characterized by the vast and arid Rakhim Desert, which stretches across its entire territory. This harsh landscape features sand dunes, salt basins, and seasonal dry wadis. Despite the extreme climate, Ricene hosts several urban centers, including the capital Meiyah, which lies on the coast of the Sea of Ricene. The Al-Karif Ridge, a low escarpment near the western border, separates Ricene from Lamberia and contains the country's main mining sites. Infrastructure development is concentrated along the Desert Artery Corridor, a major highway and pipeline network linking key settlements.
  • Toplan, the second and larger part of the Union, is a massive island nation situated in the Grandic Ocean, making it the largest island in the Tayanusan continent. Toplan features the expansive Savayra Plain, a fertile savannah biome dotted with acacia woodlands, rolling grasslands, and seasonal streams. The region benefits from a more temperate climate and supports agriculture, cattle herding, and eco-tourism. The central highlands of Toplan, known as the Makhalu Uplands, offer cooler temperatures and house the island’s main religious and academic centers.

The coastline of the Union is extensive and economically crucial. The eastern seaboard of Toplan is home to major ports such as Port Dallah and Amarayn, which serve as gateways for international trade across the Grandic Ocean. Coral reefs and protected marine zones support both fishing industries and biodiversity reserves.

The country is traversed by the Wadi Al-Qatran, a seasonal river in Ricene that historically provided water and trade routes for desert tribes. Meanwhile, Toplan is home to the Lake Abtari, a freshwater lake nestled in the southern plains, crucial for irrigation and wildlife.

Overall, the geography of the Union reflects its dual nature: Ricene is a land of desert resilience, spiritual heritage, and resource extraction, while Toplan is defined by agricultural abundance, strategic isolation, and environmental balance. Together, they form a powerful and complementary federation at the crossroads of land and sea.

Politics

Government

The Union of Ricene and Toplan operates as a federal presidential republic, established under the Constitution to ensure equal representation, shared governance, and regional balance between the two founding regions. The political system is defined by a strong central executive, a bicameral legislature, and a framework that guarantees both national unity and regional autonomy.

At the head of state is the President, who is directly elected every six years by popular vote. The President serves as commander-in-chief, oversees foreign policy, appoints ministers, and ensures the execution of federal law. He also represents the unity of the federation, symbolizing the partnership between Ricenese and Toplanese citizens. The Vice President assists the President and acts as his constitutional substitute when necessary. By design, the Vice President traditionally hails from the region opposite that of the President—preserving regional parity and federal balance. The Federal Assembly serves as the legislative body and is composed of two chambers:

  • The Council of Deputies (lower house) is elected based on population, with proportional representation from both Ricene and Toplan. It initiates legislation, approves the federal budget, and supervises the executive branch.
  • The Council of Regions (upper house) ensures equal representation from both regions, with an equal number of delegates appointed by each region’s local assembly. This chamber reviews laws for constitutional compliance, regional impact, and serves as the forum for inter-regional negotiations.

The Speaker of the Assembly, moderates joint sessions, enforces legislative procedure, and mediates disputes between the two chambers.

The judiciary is led by the Chief Justice and operates independently from the executive and legislature. The Federal Constitutional Court oversees the legality of laws, arbitrates disputes between federal and regional authorities, and protects individual rights. Civil, criminal, and administrative courts exist at both federal and regional levels.

The federation respects the autonomy of Ricene and Toplan in certain policy domains such as education, cultural affairs, religious administration, and regional development, while the federal government maintains exclusive authority over defense, foreign policy, currency, and national infrastructure.

Political parties

Despite having formal democratic structures, the political system of the Union of Ricene and Toplan operates under tight state control, with elections serving more as procedural exercises than as genuine mechanisms for political competition. While a multi-party framework exists, it is heavily regulated, and most opposition is either symbolic or co-opted within the state-approved political order.

The country’s political life is dominated by the Unionist Islamic Movement, a ruling party that combines Islamist ideology, centralist nationalism, and strong allegiance to the federal presidency. The UIM promotes a doctrine of religious unity, anti-secularism, and social order, emphasizing the role of Islam as the foundation of law, governance, and public ethics. It controls the vast majority of seats in both legislative chambers and influences the appointment of judges, governors, and key media figures.

Opposition parties are technically allowed but face significant restrictions. Their campaigns are closely monitored, media access is limited, and legal thresholds prevent most from winning significant representation. Most non-UIM parties function as loyal opposition—raising policy debates without challenging the religious or political framework. Key features of the system include:

  • State dominance of media and civil society
  • Religious oversight of political platforms, especially through regional Islamic councils
  • Limits on criticism of the Union, Islam, or the President
  • Restrictions on foreign-funded political activities or secular ideological groups

Although elections are held regularly, voter turnout is often driven by state mobilization, clerical endorsement, and patriotic appeals, rather than open political competition. Independent watchdogs and international observers have repeatedly raised concerns about electoral fairness, freedom of association, and press pluralism. In essence, political parties in the Union function within a managed political space, where Islamism, federal unity, and executive supremacy form the ideological core, and democracy is formally present but substantively constrained.

Administrative subdivisions

The Union of Ricene and Toplan is a federal state composed of two primary constituent regions: Ricene (on the mainland) and Toplan (a large island to the northeast). Each region is further divided into governorates, which serve as the core administrative units responsible for implementing federal and regional policies.

Ricene is divided into eight governorates, each centered around major urban centers, desert transport hubs, or strategic industrial zones. These governorates are governed by Walis (governors), who are appointed by the President in consultation with regional authorities. Their responsibilities include local infrastructure, public order, and coordination with religious institutions and security forces.

Toplan, the island region, is divided into six governorates, reflecting its larger geographical size and population distribution. Due to its insular nature and distinct identity, Toplan enjoys greater administrative autonomy in areas such as education, cultural heritage, and tribal governance. Its governorates are managed by regional councils, which include local elders, religious scholars, and federal appointees.

Each governorate has districts (mudiriyas) and municipalities (baladiyat), which handle everyday administration such as sanitation, civil records, and minor law enforcement. Mayors and district heads are appointed rather than elected, ensuring alignment with central government policies and religious norms. The capital city, Meiyah, holds a special federal administrative status, directly managed by the Federal Capital Authority. It hosts the main institutions of government, including the Presidential Palace, Federal Assembly, Supreme Court, and embassies.

While the Constitution grants formal powers to regional authorities, in practice, the central government maintains significant control, particularly in strategic matters like security, education standards, media regulation, and religious affairs. Regional assemblies exist but function mostly in an advisory or administrative capacity, rather than as fully independent legislative bodies. This administrative structure is designed to preserve federal unity, while recognizing regional identity, especially that of Toplan. However, it reinforces a system in which real authority flows from the federal executive, and where local governance operates under a framework of religious and political conformity.

Foreign relations

The Union of Ricene and Toplan maintains an active but strategically controlled foreign policy, shaped by its emphasis on Islamic solidarity, economic pragmatism, and the preservation of national sovereignty. As a member of the Tayanusan Community, the United Nations, and other international institutions such as the Global Reserve Fund, World Trade Network, and International Energy Agency, Ricene participates in multilateral diplomacy while maintaining a cautious stance toward foreign influence and liberal ideological models.

The country’s foreign policy is closely aligned with its Tayanusan identity and internal priorities. It promotes cooperation with other Tayanusan countries, particularly Azaria, Sifalam, and Lamberia, with whom it shares deep cultural, linguistic, and ideological ties. These relationships are reinforced through joint infrastructure projects, energy trade, religious diplomacy, and education exchanges centered on Islamic scholarship.

Despite being officially non-aligned, Ricene maintains limited engagement with Edrobean countries, particularly in areas such as energy export, shipping, and technical investment, while remaining politically distant from nations that advocate secularism or liberal democracy. Foreign companies operating in Ricene must adhere to strict regulations on content, conduct, and religious respect.

The Union plays a modest but symbolic role in inter-Islamic diplomacy, as member of the Islamic Cooperation Organization, often hosting conferences or supporting resolutions within the United Nations’s cultural and moral councils. It opposes foreign interventions in Muslim-majority nations and advocates for a global order that respects religious sovereignty, non-interference, and the moral distinctiveness of Islamic societies.

Military alliances are avoided by constitutional principle. Ricene does not host foreign troops, participate in offensive coalitions, or allow international bases on its soil. Its defense doctrine emphasizes territorial integrity, coastal security, and internal protection over projection of power. In summary, Ricene’s foreign relations are defined by strategic restraint, religious diplomacy, and selective economic engagement, aiming to preserve domestic order, protect Islamic values, and maintain a position of moral autonomy in a fragmented international system.

Military

Ricene maintains a disciplined and defense-oriented military, known as the Armed Forces of Ricene and Toplan. Its primary mission is to protect national sovereignty, defend constitutional order, and ensure internal stability across both Ricene and Toplan. The armed forces operate under the direct command of the President, who serves as Commander-in-Chief, with day-to-day operations managed by the Ministry of Defense. The military structure is unified but divided into three key branches:

  • Army – The largest component, responsible for border security, desert patrol, and internal reinforcement operations. These forces are heavily deployed in Ricene’s Rakhim Desert and along the Lamberian border, and are trained in desert warfare, urban counter-insurgency, and infrastructure defense.
  • Navy – Tasked with securing Ricene’s coastline along the Sea of Ricene and Grandic Ocean, this branch operates fast patrol boats, coastal surveillance radars, and port defense units. It is especially active around Toplan, where maritime sovereignty and anti-smuggling enforcement are priorities.
  • Air Force – Though modest in size, it plays a key role in air surveillance, defensive interception, and logistical transport between the mainland and Toplan. It includes drones, light fighters, and helicopters, many of which are domestically manufactured or imported from partner states within Tayanusa.

Military service is mandatory for men aged 18–24 for a minimum of 18 months, with exemptions granted for religious or medical reasons. The Military Academy of Meiyah and the Toplan Island Command School train officers with an emphasis on discipline, Islamic ethics, and territorial loyalty. The Union does not participate in international military alliances and forbids the presence of foreign military forces on its soil. While maintaining a policy of non-aggression, the military is prepared for rapid mobilization, particularly in response to internal unrest, border incidents, or threats to religious stability.

Military spending accounts for approximately 4% of GDP, with priority placed on technology upgrades, coastal defense, and strategic self-sufficiency. The military is widely respected and plays a visible role in national ceremonies, religious parades, and state-sponsored youth programs. In national identity, the armed forces are seen not only as guardians of territory, but as protectors of Islamic integrity, national unity, and the moral order upheld by the Union’s constitutional vision.

Law enforcement

Law enforcement in the Union is decentralized under the authority of the Ministry of Internal Security, which oversees both the local police forces and the country’s extensive intelligence apparatus. These institutions are tasked not only with maintaining public safety and order but also with upholding Islamic law, protecting national unity, and preserving the ideological framework of the state. The national police force operates throughout all governorates of Ricene and Toplan. It is divided into several specialized divisions, including:

  • Local Police, responsible for routine patrols, traffic regulation, and crime prevention
  • Federal Police, focused on major crimes, fraud, and internal security threats

Police officers are appointed and trained through a system heavily influenced by religious education, and their authority extends to both civil enforcement and moral oversight. Officers routinely collaborate with religious authorities and local clerics when handling matters of public conduct, particularly in markets, schools, and urban centers.

Parallel to the police, the Federal Directorate of Intelligence acts as the country’s primary domestic surveillance and counter-subversion agency. It monitors political dissent, foreign influence, and potential threats to the federal government. The FDI operates both openly and covertly and is known for maintaining a comprehensive database of religious preachers, student organizations, civil society actors, and political figures.

In Toplan, where tribal and regional dynamics are more complex, law enforcement often incorporates local intelligence units and militia-style auxiliaries, coordinated under the governorates but monitored by the federal center. These units focus on smuggling prevention, tribal mediation, and coastal monitoring.

Dissent or perceived disloyalty—particularly toward the federal structure —is swiftly addressed through a combination of legal action, public reprimand, and preventive detention. Freedom of expression is constitutionally limited, especially where it conflicts with religion, or national unity. Law enforcement is widely visible, present at all major public events, and often involved in civic campaigns, such as those promoting religious observance, national loyalty, or traditional family values.

Economy

The economy of the Union of Ricene and Toplan is one of the largest in Tayanusa, with a GDP of 698 billion DTR. It is a federally managed, semi-state-driven economy built on a blend of resource wealth, strategic trade, and infrastructure-focused investment, guided by Islamic economic principles and political centralization.

The energy sector forms the backbone of the economy, with Ricene possessing vast natural gas fields, rare minerals, and desert-based solar installations. State-controlled companies dominate extraction and export operations, and revenues from energy are funneled through sovereign funds to finance national infrastructure, defense, and religious institutions.

In Toplan, the economy is centered around agriculture, light manufacturing, and logistics, supported by the region’s more temperate savannah climate. The Savayra Plain is highly productive, yielding grains, livestock, and palm oil. Toplan is also home to the Port of Amarayn, one of the Union’s key maritime trade hubs for goods entering the Grandic Ocean.

The federal government enforces strict adherence to Sharia-compliant financial systems, banning interest-based banking and mandating zakat contributions from businesses and individuals. The national financial sector is dominated by Islamic banks and state-led investment groups, offering murabaha (cost-plus financing), profit-sharing models, and state-backed development bonds.

Large-scale infrastructure projects are central to the economic agenda, including desert highways, high-speed rail between coastal hubs, desalination plants, and the Desert Artery Pipeline, which carries gas and water between Ricene and Toplan. These projects serve not only as economic drivers but as instruments of national unity and regime legitimacy.

Private enterprise exists in retail, construction, and services, but is closely regulated and often politically linked. Most major industries—especially energy, media, and logistics—remain either state-owned or under loyalist control. Foreign investment is permitted in designated industrial zones but subject to ideological vetting, religious compliance, and joint ventures with state-approved partners.

Employment is heavily concentrated in the public sector, including the civil service, military, religious institutions, and state-run enterprises. Youth unemployment remains a challenge, especially in urban Ricene, prompting the expansion of technical training programs and government-backed entrepreneurship funds. Trade relations focus on Tayanusan neighbors, particularly Azaria and Sifalam, and selective Edrobean partners. The Union exports gas, solar power components, and agri-products, and imports machinery, high-tech equipment, and foodstuffs.

Demography

Language

The official and sole language of the Union of Ricene and Toplan is Tayanusan, which serves as the unifying linguistic foundation across both Ricene and Toplan despite their distinct regional identities. Tayanusan is used in all aspects of government, education, law, media, and religious institutions, and is considered not only a national standard but a moral and civic obligation for all citizens.

While Ricenese and Toplanese dialects exist, they are treated as regional oral variants and are not used in formal communication or education. The state encourages the use of standard Tayanusan across all regions to promote national unity and religious consistency. All citizens are expected to be fluent, and linguistic conformity is a requirement for employment in public service, the judiciary, and media. The Federal Language Authority, operating under the Ministry of Religious and Cultural Affairs, regulates vocabulary standards, script usage, and the publication of official literature. It also monitors foreign influence on language, ensuring that neologisms and imported expressions align with cultural and theological values.

Religion

Religion is the central pillar of national identity and governance in the Union of Ricene and Toplan, where Islam is constitutionally enshrined as the foundation of the state, law, and society. Approximately 97% of the population identifies as Muslim, predominantly Sunni, following the Tayanusan tradition of jurisprudence, which blends classical theology with state-defined orthodoxy. The remaining 3%, mostly Christian minorities, are permitted private worship but have limited visibility in public life.

The state upholds a model of federal Islamic republicanism, in which all laws, institutions, and social policies must conform to Sharia. The President is required to be Muslim, and all high-level officials, including judges, governors, and educators, must pass religious ethics evaluations supervised by the Supreme Religious Council—a federal body of ulama (Islamic scholars) who serve as ideological guardians of the Union.

Public religious life is highly structured. Friday prayers, Ramadan observance, and zakat contributions are nationally mandated and coordinated through the Ministry of Religious Affairs, which oversees all mosques, religious schools, clerical appointments, and Islamic publications. Sermons are standardized across regions and designed to reinforce themes of loyalty, moral order, and obedience to God and the federal system.

In Ricene, religion is especially intertwined with public administration and legal institutions. In Toplan, though cultural expressions of Islam may differ slightly, the religious infrastructure remains under full federal supervision, with regional councils operating as subordinate bodies to national religious authorities.

Christian communities—mostly descendants of pre-Islamic groups or Edrobean migrants—are permitted to operate licensed churches and celebrate private rites, but proselytism, religious criticism, or deviation from state religious norms are strictly prohibited. Apostasy, blasphemy, and public deviation from Islam are criminal offenses under the Code of Religious Protection.

Education across all levels includes mandatory Qur’anic studies, and Islamic jurisprudence is a required subject in law, medicine, economics, and civil service training. Youth programs, national holidays, and even military service are infused with religious rituals and messaging.

In foreign policy, religion serves as both a diplomatic tool and a boundary marker. The Union promotes pan-Islamic cooperation, funds Qur’anic education abroad, and supports religious infrastructure in allied countries, while rejecting secularism and foreign ideological influence.

Ethnicities

The Union of Ricene and Toplan is home to a diverse yet tightly integrated ethnic composition, shaped by its federal structure and unified under a shared Islamic-national identity. The population is primarily composed of Ricenese (58%) and Toplanese (36%), with the remaining 4% consisting of smaller minority groups, including tribal descendants, coastal dwellers of mixed heritage, and Edrobean migrant communities.

The Ricenese are the dominant ethnic group on the mainland, tracing their lineage to ancient desert clans and urban Islamic scholars. They are known for their traditional desert culture, religious scholarship, and leadership roles in the federal government and judiciary. Ricenese identity is strongly linked to the capital Meiyah, the Rakhim Desert, and the historical Islamic movements that originated there. They form the cultural and administrative backbone of the Union.

The Toplanese, native to the island of Toplan, have a distinct cultural expression rooted in tribal councils, pastoral customs, and a long-standing maritime tradition. Though linguistically unified under Tayanusan, the Toplanese retain unique dress, oral traditions, and spiritual interpretations that are integrated into the federal structure through autonomy arrangements and local governance councils. In recent decades, many Toplanese have risen to senior positions in the military, agriculture ministry, and regional administration.

Despite their differences, both groups are bound by the federal constitution, religious doctrine, and the state’s ideological framework, which prioritizes unity under Islam and discourages ethnic separatism. National programs focus on linguistic standardization, inter-regional marriages, and civic rituals that emphasize collective identity over ancestral distinctions.

Ethnic minorities, though few in number, are present primarily in urban coastal centers and border regions. They are fully subject to national laws and integration policies. While some retain private cultural practices, public life is expected to conform to the dominant linguistic, religious, and ideological norms.

The government does not officially recognize ethnicity as a legal tag; instead, it promotes a model of religious-national unity, where the ideal citizen is one who speaks Tayanusan, practices Islam, and adheres to the values of the Union—regardless of origin. This approach has produced a state that is ethnically diverse in origin, but ideologically and culturally homogenous by design.
